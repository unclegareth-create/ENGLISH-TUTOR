Museum Words (Passive Decoding) Receptive Only
Historical Victorian terminology. Understand their cultural connotation, but do not produce them in modern essays.
Calèche [noun] A light, low-wheeled 19th-century carriage with a folding hood. In contemporary formal writing, use carriage or vehicle.
Csoba [noun] A traditional Romanian shepherd's woollen cloak. Strictly historical/ethnographic.
Post-haste [adverb] With great speed. Modern high-register academic alternatives: promptly, expeditiously, or without delay.
Arsenal Words (Active C1/C2 Lexis) High-Yield Production
Sophisticated analytical vocabulary that elevates Cambridge discursive writing, reviews, and speaking.
Apprehensive [adjective] Anxious or fearful that something unpleasant will occur. High-utility collocations: increasingly apprehensive, apprehensive about the prospect.
Impenetrable [adjective] Impossible to pass through, see through, or comprehend. Collocates: impenetrable gloom, impenetrable prose/jargon.
Hemmed in [phrasal participle] Surrounded and restricted. High-yield in abstract essays: hemmed in by bureaucratic regulations.

2. Syntactic Architecture: Participle Clauses

C1/C2 Clause Combining

Participle clauses allow advanced writers to pack secondary information, chronology, and causality into an economical syntactic frame without endlessly compounding coordinate main clauses:

1. Present Participle (-ing)

Denotes simultaneous action, immediate consequence, or an active subject's rationale:

• Rounding the mountain spur, the carriage entered a dense fog.
(= As it rounded the spur...)

2. Past Participle (-ed / 3rd Form)

Replaces a passive clause. Economical, formal, and establishes atmospheric conditions:

• Alarmed by the howling of wolves, the horses reared up.
(= Because they were alarmed...)

3. Perfect Participle (Having + ed)

Explicitly indicates that one action was completed before the subsequent action began:

• Having secured his passport, Harker departed for Bistritz.
(= After he had secured...)

4. The Dangling Participle Trap

The implicit subject of the participle MUST match the grammatical subject of the main clause:

• ✗ Gazing into the abyss, the carriage lurched. (Carriages cannot gaze!)
• ✓ Gazing into the abyss, Harker felt the carriage lurch.
