Opening & Linking with Participles

1. Present & Absolute (-ing)Simultaneous action, reason, or with + noun + -ing → "with the afternoon sun falling full upon them..."

"I'd like to tell you about a late-autumn trip I took two years ago up to the west coast of Scotland. Having spent the best part of six months staring at spreadsheets in a cramped city-centre flat, I badly needed a few days somewhere quiet, so I booked a small stone cottage near Loch Torridon.

To get there, I had to take a single-track train line from Inverness and then catch a local bus for the final twenty miles. By the time we turned into the mountain pass, the light was fading fast, with low storm clouds rolling in off the Atlantic and cutting visibility down to a few yards. At one point the bus had to pull into a passing place because a herd of red deer was standing right in the middle of the tarmac. Looking out of the steamed-up window, I remember feeling a mixture of awe and mild apprehension—the glen was hemmed in by sheer dark crags on both sides, and my phone had lost all signal an hour earlier.

Once I finally reached the cottage and lit the stove, though, that initial unease vanished completely. The reason that journey has stuck with me ever since is that it reminded me how rarely we allow ourselves to feel genuinely small in nature. Whenever work starts to feel overwhelming now, I still picture that quiet road through the pass."

Syntax Lens:
Original Novel Extract (1897)
"Beyond the green swelling hills of the Mittel Land rose mighty slopes of forest up to the lofty steeps of the Carpathians themselves. Right and left of us they towered, with the afternoon sun falling full upon them and bringing out all the glorious colours of this beautiful range, deep blue and purple in the shadows of the peaks... Soon we were hemmed in with trees, which in places arched right over the roadway till we passed as through a tunnel; and again great frowning rocks guarded us boldly on either side. Though we were in shelter, we could hear the rising wind, for it moaned and whistled through the rocks, and the branches of the trees crashed together as we swept along." — Bram Stoker, Dracula (1897), Chapter 1: Jonathan Harker's Journal Original Text
📖 Close-Reading Notes & Verbatim Bistritz Context
1. "...with the afternoon sun falling full upon them and bringing out..." This is a C2 with + noun + participle absolute construction. Because it introduces its own subject (the afternoon sun) after with, it can attach smoothly to a main clause whose subject is they [the slopes] without creating a dangling participle.
2. "Soon we were hemmed in with trees..." Here Stoker uses a passive finite verb (were hemmed in). When we compress this into a C1 sentence opener, we drop Soon we were and start directly with the past participle: "Hemmed in by trees, we passed as through a tunnel."
3. "Beyond the green swelling hills... rose mighty slopes" Notice the place inversion opening the paragraph: Stoker fronts the prepositional phrase (Beyond the... hills) before the intransitive verb (rose) and subject (mighty slopes)—a structure you will master in Lesson 11.
Verbatim Earlier Context • Chapter 1 (Golden Krone Hotel, Bistritz, 4 May)

"Just before I was leaving, the old lady came up to my room and said in a very hysterical way: 'Must you go? Oh! young Herr, must you go?' She was in such an excited state that she seemed to have lost her grip of what German she knew, and mixed it all up with some other language which I did not know at all. I was just able to follow her by asking many questions. When I told her that I must go at once, and that I was engaged on important business, she asked again:

'Do you know what day it is?' I answered that it was the fourth of May. She shook her head as she said again: 'Oh, yes! I know that! I know that, but do you know what day it is? It is the eve of St. George's Day. Do you not know that to-night, when the clock strikes midnight, all the evil things in the world will have full sway? Do you know where you are going, and what you are going to?' She was in such evident distress that I tried to comfort her, but without effect. Finally she went down on her knees and implored me not to go; at least to wait a day or two before starting."

Period & Literary Expressions (Reading & Fiction) 1890s / Literary
Distinguish historical 19th-century nouns (calèche) from vivid literary personification (frowning rocks) that belongs in creative fiction rather than formal reports.
Calèche [archaic noun • Ch. 1] A light, four-wheeled horse-drawn carriage with a folding hood. Historical object noun.
Frowning rocks [literary personification • Ch. 1] Overhanging, threatening cliff faces described as if scowling. Vivid in Gothic fiction, too theatrical for an academic essay.
Lofty steeps [poetic plural noun • Ch. 1] High mountain precipices ("the lofty steeps of the Carpathians"). In modern prose, write steep mountain slopes or sheer cliffs.
Modern C1/C2 Words (High-Value for Exams) Use in Writing & Speaking
High-frequency C1/C2 vocabulary from this chapter and module that transfers directly into modern essays, proposals, and reviews.
Apprehensive [adjective] Anxious or uneasy that something unpleasant will happen. Natural collocations: feel apprehensive about the future, apprehensive passengers.
Impenetrable [adjective] Impossible to pass through, see through, or understand. Natural collocations: impenetrable darkness, impenetrable legal jargon.
Hemmed in (by) [past participle / phrasal verb] Surrounded closely so movement or choice is restricted. Works physically or metaphorically: hemmed in by high-rise blocks, hemmed in by rising costs.

2. The Grammar Pattern: Participle Clauses

Core • C1/C2 Sentence Architecture (3 Marks)

When I mark forty C1 or IELTS scripts before lunch, nothing puts me to sleep faster than a string of "Because... and then... after that..." sentences—and nothing makes me reach for the red pen faster than a dangling participle where a castle appears to be crossing a bridge. Master these four patterns, and three well-placed participles will wake your examiner right up:

1. Present Participle (-ing)

[ (Not) + Verb-ing + Object ], [ Same Subject + Main Verb ]

Expresses the reason for an action or two actions happening simultaneously. For negatives, place Not right at the front:

• Sensing the passengers' fear, the driver urged the horses on.
• Not wanting to alarm his fellow travellers, Jonathan stayed silent.

2. Past Participle (-ed / V3)

[ Past Participle + by/in/with ], [ Passive Subject + Verb ]

Turns a passive finite clause ("The road was hemmed in by trees") into a concise passive opener:

• Hemmed in by dark pine trees, the road felt like a tunnel.
• Filled with apprehension, Harker gripped the carriage door.

3. Perfect Participle (Having + V3)

[ Having + (been) + V3 ], [ Same Subject + Main Verb ]

Explicitly foregrounds sequence when the first action finished before the main clause began:

• Having crossed the bridge, we entered the pass. (Active)
• Having been warned about the pass, Harker felt uneasy. (Passive)

4. The Same-Subject Rule

✗ Dangling Participle Trap

For an unattached participle opener, the noun immediately after the comma must perform (or receive) that action:

• ✗ Crossing the bridge, the castle appeared ahead.
• ✓ Crossing the bridge, we saw the castle ahead.
🎓 C2 Nuance: Two Legitimate Exceptions to the Same-Subject Rule
1. With + Noun + Participle (Absolute Construction): In Stoker's sentence—"Right and left of us they towered, with the afternoon sun falling full upon them"—the participle has its own explicit subject (the afternoon sun) introduced by with. Useful in C1/C2 reports: "With housing costs rising rapidly, many graduates are delaying marriage."
2. Fixed C2 Discourse Participles: Established comment clauses such as Generally speaking, Broadly speaking, Judging by the latest figures, and Weather permitting, are accepted idioms that do not require the speaker to be the subject after the comma.

3. Cambridge Reading and Use of English Part 4: Key Word Transformations

Core • 2 Marks Each (10 Marks Total)

Complete the second sentence so that it has a similar meaning to the first sentence, using the word given. Do not change the word given. Use between 3 and 6 words, including the key word (the strict C1 Advanced limit; C2 Proficiency permits up to 8, though every question below fits cleanly in 4–6 words).

🧭 The 4-Step Part 4 Deconstruction Method (Click "🔍 4-Step Breakdown" on any question):
1. Strike Out Echoes: Cross out words from Sentence 1 already repeated in Sentence 2. 2. Tense Harmony: Check whether Action 1 happened before Action 2 (Having + V3) or simultaneously. 3. Voice Shift: Check whether the subject after the comma is active or passive (Having been + V3). 4. Fit the Pattern: Build the 3–6 word phrase without altering the key word (1+1 split marks awarded).
8. After the villagers told him of the dangers along the pass, Harker felt increasingly uneasy.
WARNED
1. Strike Out Echoes: After the villagers told him of the dangers along the pass, Harker felt increasingly uneasy.
2. Tense & Lexis Shift: "told him of [dangers]" prior to the main verb → warned about / of.
3. Voice Shift: Harker is the subject after the comma; he received the warning → Passive perfect participle (Having been warned...).
4. Pattern (4 words): Having been warned about (also accepts Having been warned of or Warned by the villagers about).
the dangers along the pass, Harker felt increasingly uneasy.
9. Because he had no wish to frighten his fellow travellers, Jonathan hid his fear.
ALARM
1. Strike Out Echoes: Because he had no wish to frighten his fellow travellers, Jonathan hid his fear.
2. Tense & Lexis Shift: "had no wish to frighten" → negative participle opener + to alarm.
3. Voice Shift: Active (Jonathan did not want to alarm them). Place Not before the -ing participle.
4. Pattern (4 words): Not wanting to alarm (also accepts Not wishing to alarm or So as not to alarm).
his fellow travellers, Jonathan hid his fear.
10. The road was closely surrounded by dense pine forests and received very little sunlight.
HEMMED
1. Strike Out Echoes: The road was closely surrounded by dense pine forests and received very little sunlight.
2. Tense Harmony: Passive state describing the road → Past participle opener.
3. Voice Shift: Passive (The road is surrounded) → Hemmed in by...
4. Pattern (6 words): Hemmed in by dense pine forests
, the road received very little sunlight.
11. Because he felt deep anxiety about what lay ahead, Harker gripped the side of the carriage.
APPREHENSION
1. Strike Out Echoes: Because he felt deep anxiety about what lay ahead, Harker gripped the side of the carriage.
2. Nominalisation Shift: "felt deep anxiety" shifts to the past participle + noun collocation Filled with apprehension.
3. Preposition Link: apprehension about / at what lay [ahead].
4. Pattern (6 words): Filled with apprehension about what lay
ahead, Harker gripped the side of the carriage.
12. The courtyard fell into complete darkness that was impossible to see through when the clouds covered the moon.
IMPENETRABLE
1. Strike Out Echoes: The courtyard fell into complete darkness that was impossible to see through when the clouds covered the moon.
2. Tense Harmony: Past passive (The courtyard was...) + past participle plunged.
3. Collocation & Subordinator: plunged into impenetrable darkness + temporal conjunction when / as the (before the finite clause clouds covered the moon).
4. Pattern (6 words): plunged into impenetrable darkness when the
The courtyard was clouds covered the moon.
