IELTS Speaking Part 3: How to Give Extended, Nuanced Answers (Band 7 to 8+)

Part 3 is where the examiner makes up their mind. By the time you finish your two-minute monologue in Part 2, the examiner has a working number in their head. Part 3 is where that number gets confirmed—or promoted to a Band 7.5 or 8.0.

If your instinct is to answer like you're chatting in a coffee shop (“Well, last week my cousin bought a car...”), your score hits a glass ceiling. Part 3 is not an interrogation about your private life; it is an academic conversation about society, trends, and cause-and-effect.

Here is the practical A-R-E-E framework I drill with my students, authentic Cambridge model answers, and the conversational pivots that make you sound unhurried and articulate under pressure.

Why Part 3 is the turning point

Part 3 lasts four to five minutes. While Part 1 focuses on familiar personal topics (your hometown, your routine) and Part 2 is an uninterrupted two-minute monologue, Part 3 shifts into an analytical discussion. The examiner asks broad, societal questions that demand:

  • Abstract thinking: Looking beyond your personal bubble to consider society, trends, demographics, and future developments.
  • Hypothetical speculation: Using conditionals and modal verbs (might, could, would have) to evaluate possibilities.
  • Sustained discourse: Developing an argument across three to six sentences without running out of steam or repeating yourself.

The A-R-E-E Framework for 30-Second Answers

When students struggle with Part 3, they either give a clipped two-sentence answer or wander aimlessly for a minute until the examiner cuts them off. To hit Band 7 or 8, use this reliable four-step architecture:

1 A – Answer (Direct Stance with Hedging)

State your position immediately, but avoid simplistic absolutes. Use nuanced hedges like “Broadly speaking, I'd say that...” or “It really depends on the demographic, but on the whole...”.

2 R – Rationale (Why this happens)

Unpack the underlying mechanism. Explain the economic, social, technological, or psychological reason driving the trend.

3 E – Example or Comparison

Ground your point with an objective illustration. Compare younger vs. older generations, past vs. present, or urban vs. rural settings.

4 E – Extension or Consequence

Conclude with a consequence or future projection: “Consequently, this could mean that...” or “That said, it isn't necessarily a negative shift.”

Side-by-Side: Band 6 vs Band 7.5 vs Band 8.5

Let's look at how answers differ in practice when responding to an authentic Cambridge examiner question:

“Do you think people read fewer books today than they did in the past?”
Band 6.0 Answer Short & overly personal

“Yes, I think so. People use their smartphones all the time now, so they don't have time to read books. For example, my brother spends five hours a day on social media and never reads anything.”

Examiner note: Only two sentences. Too reliant on personal anecdote (“my brother”) rather than societal analysis. Basic linking and limited vocabulary range.

Band 7.0 – 7.5 Answer Extended & balanced

“I'd say so, at least when it comes to traditional printed literature. Smart devices definitely consume the downtime that people used to devote to novels. That being said, it could be argued that reading hasn't declined so much as evolved. People consume a tremendous volume of written material every day – articles, newsletters, and online forums – so literacy habits have simply shifted format.”

Examiner note: Demonstrates qualification (“at least when it comes to...”), contrastive linking (“That being said...”), and sophisticated collocations (“devote to novels”, “shifted format”).

Band 8.5 Answer Analytical, nuanced & idiomatic

“It's nuanced, but on balance, yes – particularly regarding long-form fiction. The algorithmic pull of micro-content has substantially shortened our collective attention spans, making deep, sustained reading far more of an uphill battle. Having said that, audiobooks and digital readers have seen exponential uptake, so while physical paperbacks might be gathering dust on shelves, our engagement with storytelling itself hasn't necessarily evaporated.”

Examiner note: Effortless discourse markers (“on balance”, “Having said that”), natural idiomatic phrases (“uphill battle”, “gathering dust on shelves”), and sophisticated abstract reasoning.

Discourse Markers That Sound Natural (Not Rehearsed)

Memorised templates like “That is a very interesting question that touches upon a multi-faceted dilemma” make examiners cringe. Native speakers don't talk like that. Instead, learn flexible, natural discourse markers:

To Buy Thinking Time Naturally

  • • “That's quite a tough one to generalise about, but...”
  • • “Off the top of my head, I'd say...”
  • • “If you look at the broader picture...”

To Qualify or Introduce Nuance

  • • “Up to a point, certainly, but it depends on...”
  • • “That being said, there is another angle to this...”
  • • “I'd hazard a guess that older generations view this differently...”

Four Mistakes That Cap You at Band 6

  1. Sticking to personal anecdotes: If asked “Why do people enjoy shopping?”, do not reply “Well, last Saturday I bought a jacket at Zara.” Talk about consumer psychology, social leisure, or retail convenience.
  2. Over-rehearsed clichés: Phrases like “Every coin has two sides” or “Since the dawn of history” sound artificial and demonstrate memorisation rather than spontaneous fluency.
  3. Monotone intonation: Band 7+ requires expressive pronunciation. Vary your pitch to emphasise key contrasts (e.g. “It isn't that reading has stopped, it's that it has transformed”).
  4. Drifting off topic: When extending your answer, ensure your fourth sentence explicitly ties back to the examiner's core question.

Try It Yourself: Exam Practice Prompts

Record your voice answering these three prompts. Aim for 30 to 45 seconds per response using the A-R-E-E framework:

Prompt 1: What impact has remote working had on family life? See strategy →

Recommended Strategy: Explore the double-edged sword. On one hand, parents save commute time and share meals. On the other, the boundary between professional stress and domestic relaxation blurs.

Key Vocab: work-life integration, blurred boundaries, commute fatigue, domestic equilibrium.

Prompt 2: Should governments invest more in public parks or shopping centres? See strategy →

Recommended Strategy: Emphasise public health vs commercial revenue. Commercial centres generate tax income and private employment, but green spaces are essential for mental wellbeing and urban biodiversity.

Key Vocab: urban green canopy, civic infrastructure, commercial viability, mental resilience.
