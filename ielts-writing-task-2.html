IELTS Writing Task 2: The 4-Paragraph Framework Across All 5 Question Types (With Model Answer)

When an IELTS candidate comes to my desk having sat Writing three or four times with their score stuck stubbornly at 6.5, the first thing I ask to see is their scrap paper. Invariably, it is blank. They opened the booklet and started writing forty seconds later.

They don't have a vocabulary problem. They have a planning and composure problem. When you don't know where a paragraph is going to land before you begin writing it, your argument wanders, your syntax gets tied in knots, and the examiner clocks the lack of direction by sentence three.

Here is the robust four-paragraph skeleton I have taught across nineteen years in the classroom—including fifteen years lecturing at Belfast Metropolitan College—how it flexes across all five IELTS essay types, and a full Band 8.5 model you can study.

What you are actually being marked on

Task 2 asks for an essay of at least 250 words, and you should spend about 40 minutes on it. It counts for twice as much as Task 1, so it deserves the larger share of your time. Examiners score it against four equally weighted criteria:

  • Task Response – Did you answer every part of the question, with a clear position that runs through the whole essay?
  • Coherence and Cohesion – Is it logically organised, with one central idea per paragraph and linking that feels natural rather than mechanical?
  • Lexical Resource – Are your word choices precise and varied, with accurate collocations?
  • Grammatical Range and Accuracy – Do you use a mix of complex structures, and are most sentences error-free?

Notice that two of the four criteria are about organisation and argument, not language. That is why structure matters so much.

The four-paragraph framework (and how it adapts to all 5 question types)

Every IELTS Task 2 essay uses the same four-paragraph skeleton (Introduction → Body 1 → Body 2 → Conclusion). Two well-developed body paragraphs almost always score better than three or four thin ones because examiners reward depth: an idea that is explained and supported with a concrete example.

However, what goes inside Body 1 and Body 2 changes depending on which of the 5 IELTS question types you are given. Select a question type below to see the exact paragraph-by-paragraph blueprint:

Type 1: Opinion Essay (Agree or Disagree)

Prompt: “To what extent do you agree or disagree?”

1. Introduction (2–3 sentences)

Paraphrase the statement + state a clear, direct opinion (e.g., “I completely agree that...” or “While there is some merit to X, I largely disagree because...”).

2. Body Paragraph 1

First main reason supporting your opinion → explain why → give a specific real-world example → link back to your position.

3. Body Paragraph 2

Second main reason supporting your opinion (or acknowledge a concession + show why your view still holds) → explain → specific example.

4. Conclusion (2 sentences)

Restate your exact opinion in fresh words and summarise your two reasons. Never introduce a new argument here.

Examiner Trap: Sitting on the fence (“50/50”) without a clear decision, or saying “I completely agree” in the intro and then writing a whole paragraph agreeing with the other side.

Type 2: Discussion + Opinion

Prompt: “Discuss both views and give your own opinion.”

1. Introduction (2–3 sentences)

Paraphrase both views + state clearly which side you agree with (e.g., “While some argue X, I believe Y is far more convincing.”).

2. Body Paragraph 1 (The Other Side)

Discuss the view you do NOT agree with first. Use attribution (“Proponents of X argue that...”) → explain their logic → give an example.

3. Body Paragraph 2 (Your Side)

Pivot to the view you DO agree with (“However, I side with those who believe...”) → explain why it is stronger → give a specific example.

4. Conclusion (2 sentences)

Weigh both sides briefly and reaffirm your own opinion clearly (see the full model answer below for this exact structure in action).

Examiner Trap: Discussing both sides neutrally and only dropping your opinion into the final sentence—examiners want a clear position throughout the essay.

Type 3: Advantages & Disadvantages

Prompt: “Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?”

1. Introduction (2–3 sentences)

Paraphrase the trend + state the main benefit and drawback (and if the prompt asks “outweigh”, state clearly which side is heavier).

2. Body Paragraph 1

The Disadvantages (or weaker side): Focus on one or two clear drawbacks → explain the negative impact → give a concrete example.

3. Body Paragraph 2

The Advantages (or stronger side): Show the major benefits → explain why they outweigh the drawbacks → give a concrete example.

4. Conclusion (2 sentences)

Summarise the main advantage and disadvantage, and restate which side outweighs the other.

Examiner Trap: Writing a laundry list of 4 un-explained pros and 4 un-explained cons instead of developing 1–2 core points per paragraph with depth.

Type 4: Problem & Solution (or Cause & Solution)

Prompt: “What problems does this cause, and how can they be solved?”

1. Introduction (2–3 sentences)

Paraphrase the phenomenon + briefly name the main problem/cause and the practical solution you will propose.

2. Body Paragraph 1 (Problems OR Causes)

Explain 1–2 primary problems (or root causes—check which word the prompt uses!) → explain the mechanism → give a real-world example.

3. Body Paragraph 2 (Solutions)

Propose 1–2 specific solutions that directly fix the problems in Body 1. Name who acts (governments, schools, employers) and how.

4. Conclusion (2 sentences)

Summarise the main problem and reiterate how your proposed measure resolves it.

Examiner Trap: Confusing Causes (why it happens) with Problems (negative effects), or proposing vague solutions like “the government should raise awareness.”

Type 5: Two-Part Direct Question (Double Question)

Prompt: A statement followed by two separate questions

1. Introduction (2–3 sentences)

Paraphrase the topic statement + give a brief, direct one-sentence answer to both Question 1 and Question 2.

2. Body Paragraph 1 (Answer Question 1)

Topic sentence directly answering the first question (e.g., “Why do people do this?”) → explanation → specific example.

3. Body Paragraph 2 (Answer Question 2)

Topic sentence directly answering the second question (e.g., “Is this a positive or negative development?”) → explanation → specific example.

4. Conclusion (2 sentences)

Summarise your answers to both questions in one cohesive closing paragraph.

Examiner Trap: Spending 80% of the essay on Question 1 and only adding two rushed sentences on Question 2. Give each question its own full body paragraph.

Plan for five minutes – really

It feels counter-intuitive to sit with your arms folded for five minutes while the exam clock is ticking and everyone around you is furiously scribbling. But I promise you: the candidates who start scribbling in minute one are the ones frantically crossing out half a page in minute twenty-five.

Five minutes of quiet planning buys you thirty-five minutes of calm, unhurried writing. Before your pen touches the lined answer sheet, write down four things on your question paper:

  • The question type: Are you being asked for an opinion, a balanced discussion, advantages vs disadvantages, or two direct answers? Check every prompt clause.
  • Your one-sentence thesis: What do you believe? Write it down in one crisp sentence. That is your compass for the next 270 words.
  • Body 1 anchor: Your primary argument and one specific, concrete real-world example.
  • Body 2 anchor: Your secondary argument (or counter-pivot) and its supporting real-world evidence.

Five mistakes that land on my desk every week

  1. The Half-Answer: Prompts that say “Discuss both views and give your opinion” have three distinct tasks, not two. Candidates frequently spend 240 words exploring both sides, forget their own opinion until a rushed sentence in the conclusion, and wonder why Task Response drops straight to Band 6.0.
  2. The Shifting Thesis: Announcing that you “completely agree” in your opening paragraph, only to spend Body 2 explaining why the opposite view makes total sense. If you tell the examiner you're turning left, don't suddenly veer right halfway down the road.
  3. The Robot Introduction: Memorised internet openers like “This is a highly controversial issue in today's fast-paced society...” Examiners mark forty scripts before lunch. Canned template sentences scream that you do not trust your own English. Toss them out. Paraphrase the prompt simply and state your position.
  4. Sellotape Linking: Forcing Furthermore, Moreover, and In addition onto the front of every single sentence. Real coherence comes from the logical progression of your thoughts, not from slapping linguistic glue between unrelated ideas. Let your sentences connect through meaning and reference.
  5. The Panic Shortfall (Under 250 words): Writing 243 words triggers an immediate penalty. Aim for 265–285 words. That gives you ample room to develop your examples in depth without writing so much that you run out of time to proofread your verb agreements and plurals.

Model answer (Type 2: Discuss Both Views + Opinion)

Question: Some people believe that technology has made learning easier and more effective, while others argue that it has made students more easily distracted and less able to concentrate. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Digital technology is now present in almost every classroom, and opinion is divided over whether it has improved learning or undermined students' ability to focus. While I accept that devices can be a source of distraction, I believe that, used with clear guidance, technology has made learning considerably more effective.

Those who are critical of classroom technology point to the constant interruptions that smartphones and laptops bring. A student researching a history assignment online is only ever one click away from social media, and notifications can break concentration repeatedly within a single lesson. Over time, critics argue, this trains young people to skim rather than read deeply, which weakens precisely the sustained attention that serious study requires.

However, this argument identifies a problem of management rather than of technology itself. When devices are used purposefully, the benefits are substantial. Learners can access high-quality explanations at any time, revisit difficult material at their own pace, and receive instant feedback through interactive exercises. A language student, for instance, can hear authentic pronunciation, practise with adaptive quizzes, and have their written work checked in minutes rather than waiting a week for a teacher's comments. These are advantages that a textbook alone simply cannot offer.

In conclusion, although the risk of distraction is real, it can be reduced through sensible rules and good teaching. On balance, I am convinced that technology has made learning easier and more effective, provided that students are taught to use it with discipline.

279 words

Why this answer works

  • Every part of the question is answered: the first view, the second view, and a clear opinion that stays the same from the first paragraph to the last.
  • Each body paragraph has one central idea, supported by a specific example (the history assignment; the language student).
  • The turn in paragraph three (“this argument identifies a problem of management rather than of technology itself”) is a natural way to move from the opposing view to your own.
  • Precise collocations: sustained attention, break concentration, used purposefully, instant feedback, on balance.
  • Grammatical range without showing off: a conditional (“provided that”), a concessive clause (“although the risk is real”), and relative clauses, all used accurately.

Frequently Asked Questions: IELTS Writing Task 2

How many paragraphs should an IELTS Writing Task 2 essay have? +

Four paragraphs (Introduction, Body Paragraph 1, Body Paragraph 2, and Conclusion) is the optimal structure for all 5 IELTS Task 2 question types. Two deeply developed body paragraphs with clear explanations and concrete examples score higher in Task Response than three or four thin paragraphs.

What is the best word count for IELTS Writing Task 2? +

The official minimum is 250 words. Aim for 265 to 285 words—enough space to develop two arguments in depth while leaving 3 to 4 minutes at the end to proofread grammar and collocations.

Where should I state my opinion in a “Discuss Both Views and Give Your Opinion” essay? +

State your opinion clearly in your introduction thesis, discuss the opposing view first in Body Paragraph 1, pivot to the view you support in Body Paragraph 2, and reaffirm your verdict in the conclusion.
