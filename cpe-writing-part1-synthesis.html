Stage 1 • Read Both Contrasting Passages

Urban Heritage vs Living Cities

Read both passages closely before writing a single word. Notice the exact scope: both writers debate strict historic conservation rules in cities, not architecture or housing policy in the abstract.

Text 1 96 words

Preserving the Stone

Historic streetscapes do far more than flatter visitors with postcard views; they anchor a city's collective memory and keep local craftsmanship visible in daily life. Once a Victorian terrace or stone market hall is demolished, no glass replacement can recover the accumulated social history embedded in its walls. Strict conservation rules also restrain speculative developers from replacing durable architecture with disposable buildings built for quick profit. Far from resisting progress, careful restoration teaches each generation that a city belongs to its residents over centuries rather than to whichever property cycle happens to dominate a single decade.

Text 2 94 words

Cities Are Not Museums

Treating entire districts as untouchable heritage zones comes at a steep social price. When planning boards block dense housing and modern transit upgrades to protect ageing facades, rents surge and young families are steadily priced out of the neighbourhoods where they grew up. A historic centre emptied of working residents and turned into an open-air gallery for tourists loses the very community life that made it worth saving. Sensible urban planning should protect genuinely exceptional landmarks while allowing ordinary streets to adapt, expand, and serve the practical needs of people living in them today.

Cambridge Part 1 Task Instruction: Write an essay summarising and evaluating the key points from both texts. Use your own words throughout as far as possible, and include your own ideas in your answer. Write your answer in 240 to 280 words.

Stage 4 • Annotated Band 5 / 5 Cambridge Model

Band 5/5 Synthesis Model Essay (254 Words • 20 / 20)

Zero lifted 3-word phrases from either source text, natural British English spelling, and restrained C2 syntax throughout. Toggle any Cambridge subscale below to inspect exactly how each mark is earned.

254 words • 0 lifted phrases

Both passages examine the growing tension between conserving historic urban fabric and adapting cities to contemporary demand, yet they reach sharply different conclusions about where planning priorities should lie.

The first writer contends that older architecture sustains shared civic identity and traditional workmanship in ways modern glass structures can never replicate. Rigorous preservation controls are also defended as a vital restraint on speculative redevelopment that puts short-term profit ahead of enduring stewardship. There is considerable weight to this case: once distinctive streetscapes vanish, neighbourhoods quickly acquire an interchangeable, anonymous character. Nevertheless, treating age alone as proof of merit risks defending undistinguished structures simply because they happen to have survived.

By contrast, the second passage warns that sweeping heritage restrictions curb housing supply and transport improvements, pushing up rents until younger inhabitants are steadily displaced. It further observes that centres hollowed out into picturesque backdrops for visitors forfeit the everyday neighbourhood life that gave them value, calling instead for selective protection. This critique is hard to dismiss, especially in major cities where rigid zoning frequently places visual uniformity above basic affordability. Even so, loosening regulations indiscriminately can invite hasty construction that enriches investors while leaving ordinary citizens with lifeless, poorly built quarters.

Ultimately, both perspectives point towards disciplined selectivity rather than an all-or-nothing choice. Cities are neither disposable commercial investments nor frozen relics; they thrive when landmarks of genuine architectural or cultural importance are firmly safeguarded, while everyday streets retain the flexibility to welcome new neighbours, absorb modern infrastructure, and remain genuinely liveable over time.

Stage 5 • Red-Pen Precision Drills

Six C2 Sentence-Surgery Drills

These six errors are the exact recurring slips that pull strong C2 Part 1 essays down from Band 5 to Band 3. Fix each sentence mentally (or aloud) before checking the Cambridge model.

Drill 1 • Scope Drift & Zero Article ("Modern Society")

Flawed draft: "Both texts deal with the role of cities in the modern society and how buildings have changed since the past."

C2 Repair: "Both passages examine the tension between conserving historic urban architecture and adapting modern cities to current housing and transport needs." (Drop "the" before general "modern society" and narrow immediately to the exact debate of both texts.)
Drill 2 • False Friends ("Historic" vs "Historical", "Deceivable" vs "Deceptive")

Flawed draft: "Protecting every historical facade may look appealing, yet blanket preservation offers a deceivable promise of urban vitality."

C2 Repair: "Protecting every historic facade may look appealing, yet blanket preservation offers a deceptive promise of urban vitality." (Historic = notable or important in history; historical = relating to the study of history. Deceivable means gullible; a misleading promise is deceptive.)
Drill 3 • Blended Idioms ("Venture So Far As To Say" & "Far From That")

Flawed draft: "Conservation may sound harmless, yet it is far from that, and I would venture so far as to say that rigid rules hollow out living districts."

C2 Repair: "Conservation may sound harmless, yet it is far from it, and I would go so far as to say (or venture to say) that rigid rules hollow out living districts." (Keep the two idioms separate and always finish the fixed contrast with far from it.)
Drill 4 • Uncountable Nouns & Quantifier Agreement

Flawed draft: "When affordable housings are restricted, a great amount of residents have little choice but to leave."

C2 Repair: "When affordable housing is restricted, a large number of residents (or a great many residents) have little choice but to leave." (Housing is uncountable and takes a singular verb; countable plural people take number of or many, never amount of.)
Drill 5 • Connector Calque ("Instead,") & Invented Source Claims

Flawed draft: "Instead, the second text claims that the rise of remote working has turned historic districts into tourist galleries."

C2 Repair: "By contrast, the second text argues that over-broad conservation rules and rising rents have turned historic districts into tourist galleries." (Use By contrast, or Conversely, to compare two passages; save outside ideas like remote working strictly for your own evaluation.)
Drill 6 • Preposition & Clause Precision

Flawed draft: "Selective planning brings relief on the short run, unlike some believe, and should open planners' eyes on everyday affordability."

C2 Repair: "Selective planning brings relief in the short term, contrary to what some believe, and should open planners' eyes to everyday affordability." (Always in the short/long term; follow a clause with contrary to what rather than preposition unlike; open someone's eyes to.)
