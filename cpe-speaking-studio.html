Cambridge C2 Proficiency (CPE) • Paper 4 Speaking • 16 Minutes

Cambridge C2 Proficiency Speaking Studio

You take the C2 Proficiency Speaking test with one other candidate (occasionally two) in front of two examiners: the interlocutor, who runs the conversation, and the assessor, who listens and awards the analytic marks. I built this studio around the exact 16-minute structure so you can practise every turn under realistic timing.

Official Cambridge C2 Proficiency Speaking format (16 minutes total)
Part Timing What happens in the room What earns Band 5
Part 1: Interview 2 minutes The interlocutor asks each candidate short questions about background, habits, and future plans. Natural social English that lifts a personal detail into a broader observation within 20 to 30 seconds.
Part 2: Collaborative Task 4 minutes Phase 1 (1 min) speculating on one or two photographs; Phase 2 (3 min) evaluating all photos and reaching a joint decision. Speculating rather than describing, sharing the floor, challenging diplomatically, and steering towards closure.
Part 3: Long Turn & Discussion 10 minutes Each candidate speaks alone for 2 minutes from a prompt card, the partner responds for roughly 45 seconds, and all three debate the wider theme. Signposted abstract argument, analytical partner evaluation (never flat agreement), and controlled syntax.

How Cambridge marks your speaking

The assessor grades five criteria: Grammatical Resource, Lexical Resource, Discourse Management, Pronunciation, and Interactive Communication. The interlocutor adds one Global Achievement score.

The mindset that wins C2 marks

Treat the room as a conversation between articulate adults rather than a quiz with hidden right answers. Examiners reward clear thinking aloud, responsive listening, and three or four well-placed structures over forced vocabulary.

Part 1 • Interview (2 minutes)

The Personal-to-Macro Elevation Shift

Interlocutors open with straightforward questions about where you live, what you do, or how you spend your time. Keep each answer to 20 to 30 seconds: start with a direct personal anchor, lift it briefly to a wider observation, and stop cleanly before rambling.

Answer, then extend

Give a direct answer, add one sharp reason, and ground it with a concrete detail. Two or three controlled sentences are plenty.

Drop rehearsed speeches

Examiners spot memorised biographies within five seconds. A crisp, spontaneous reply with natural rhythm outscores a recited paragraph every time.

Vary your opening

If asked "What do you enjoy about...?", resist echoing "What I enjoy about..." on every turn. Open directly and shift register cleanly.

Elevation Frame Personal Point of Departure → Wider Social Parallel → Concise Evaluation

B2 / C1 Conversational Plateau

"I try to go to the gym three times a week because it reduces my stress and keeps me healthy."

Purely personal, predictable linking with because / and, and everyday phrasing.

C2 Natural Elevation

"Given how sedentary desk work has become, carving out time for regular exercise feels less like a hobby and more like an essential counterweight to mental fatigue."

Fronted participle clause (Given how...), contrastive balance (less like X and more like Y), crisp collocation.

Drill 4 • Turn Description Into Speculation (Picture C)
"In picture C there is a man at a desk. It is late at night and he is watching a video on his laptop."
Upgrade this flat description using modal speculation and visual evidence.
Cambridge Model: "He looks as though he has been trying to master a tricky skill after a full day at work. He must have turned to online tutorials for flexibility, yet judging by his posture, he might well be finding solitary study a little isolating without anyone on hand to answer questions."
Diplomatic Challenge I take your point (about X), but wouldn't you say that + [counter-argument]?
Drill 5 • Challenge Your Partner Politely
Your partner says: "I think Picture C is the best example. You can learn absolutely anything online now."
Acknowledge the strength of online access, push back gently using Picture D or F, and invite your partner back in.
Cambridge Model: "I take your point about how accessible online tutorials have become, but wouldn't you say that something vital goes missing when there is nobody beside you to spot small mistakes? Picture D shows a kind of hands-on apprenticeship that a screen cannot really replace, and Picture F captures lifelong learning far more vividly. How do you feel about pairing F against C?"
Part 3 • Individual Long Turn & Discussion (10 minutes)

The 2-Minute Abstract Long Turn

You receive a card carrying one abstract question and three optional ideas. There is no formal note-taking minute in CPE Part 3: scan the card for 8 to 10 seconds, frame the underlying tension, develop two or three signposted points, and keep speaking until the interlocutor thanks you.

The Official Part 3 Sequence

  1. Candidate A speaks uninterrupted for 2 minutes.
  2. The interlocutor asks Candidate B a related evaluation question (~45 seconds to 1 minute).
  3. A brief follow-up to both candidates.
  4. Candidate B receives a fresh prompt card and the pattern repeats.
  5. Both candidates join a wider thematic discussion (~4 minutes).

Shaping Your 2 Minutes Without Listing

  • Frame (0:00 to 0:20): Define the central distinction instead of jumping straight into bullet 1.
  • Weave Two or Three Angles (0:20 to 1:40): Treat the bullets as springboards; connect tensions between them rather than ticking them off mechanically.
  • Land the Verdict (1:40 to 2:00): Synthesise what the tension ultimately comes down to.

CPE-Specific Skill • The 45-Second Partner Evaluation

Going Beyond "I Agree With My Partner"

The moment your partner finishes their 2-minute monologue, the interlocutor turns directly to you for a brief follow-up (roughly 45 seconds). Repeating your partner's points wastes the turn; Cambridge wants to hear that you listened closely and can extend, qualify, or reframe their argument.

The Common Trap

"I agree completely with what she said about phones taking away our time to read. It is definitely true that social media is very distracting nowadays."

Merely echoes the partner's conclusion with zero fresh nuance or structural control.

The 3-Move 45-Second Blueprint

  1. Acknowledge precisely: "While my partner rightly pointed out..."
  2. Spot the unexamined tension: "...what remains open to question is whether..."
  3. Offer a crisp alternative angle: "One could equally argue that..."
Partner Drill A • After Card 1 (Social Influence)
"Your partner argued that social media makes people conform more than ever. Do you think people are more or less independent in their thinking than they used to be?"
Cambridge Model: "While my partner made a compelling point about online peer pressure, I am not convinced earlier generations were genuinely freer in their thinking. In smaller communities fifty years ago, unspoken family and neighbourhood expectations could be just as rigid. What has changed is less the existence of conformity than the sheer speed at which trends travel, though the same networks also give curious people access to dissenting perspectives they would never have encountered before."
Partner Drill B • After Card 2 (Deep Reading)
"To what extent do you agree that digital media has permanently weakened our capacity for sustained, reflective reading?"
Cambridge Model: "While my partner rightly highlighted how constant notifications break our focus, I would argue the shift is less a permanent loss of ability and more a change of habit. People still concentrate intensely on long podcasts, complex essays, or demanding work documents when the subject genuinely grips them. What remains unexamined is whether schools and households still carve out quiet, unhurried stretches where long-form reading can actually take root."
Partner Drill C • After Card 3 (Media & Belief)
"Is it primarily the responsibility of schools to teach young people to question what they read and watch?"
Use A.R.E.A. (Assertion, Reason, Evidence, Alternative) and include one negative inversion (Rarely do...).
Cambridge Model: "Certainly, I would place it near the top of a modern curriculum. Rarely do teenagers encounter news today that has not already been curated by automated social feeds, so learning to weigh sources is now a basic civic skill. Even a simple classroom exercise comparing how two outlets frame the same story can sharpen their judgement quickly. That said, schools cannot shoulder the task alone; sceptical questioning has to be modelled around the kitchen table as well."
Grammatical Resource • Restraint Over Showing Off

Three Spoken C2 Structures That Carry Real Weight

You do not need ornate grammar in every sentence. Three or four clean structural shifts across 16 minutes (one cleft, one inversion, one participle clause, one passive report) demonstrate complete control without ever sounding rehearsed.

Upgrade 1 • Negative Inversion (SELDOM / RARELY)
Upgrade: "People rarely appreciate how demanding independent work actually is over the long run."
Formula: Seldom + auxiliary + subject + bare infinitive
Cambridge Model: "Seldom do newcomers to freelance work appreciate the steady personal toll that unpredictable income can take over several years."
Upgrade 2 • Participle Result Clause (THEREBY)
Upgrade: "Rigid workplace rules remove personal initiative and as a result people lose interest in their craft."
Formula: Main clause, thereby + -ing clause
Cambridge Model: "Overly rigid workplace procedures strip staff of genuine ownership over their decisions, thereby eroding the pride they take in their craft."
Upgrade 3 • Passive Reporting (It + passive + that / Subject + passive + to have)
Upgrade: "Lots of people think that instant messaging has made us less patient in daily life."
Try both the impersonal It is widely believed... version and the subject-fronted infinitive version.
Cambridge Model: "It is widely believed that instant messaging has made us less patient in daily life." / "Instant messaging is widely believed to have made us less patient in daily life."
Exam-Day Composure • Final Checklist

Walking Into the Exam Room Calmly

Part 1 (2 mins)

  • Answer, reason, concrete detail.
  • 20 to 30 seconds, then stop cleanly.
  • Lift one personal detail towards a wider view.

Part 2 (4 mins)

  • Speculate; never give a shopping list of objects.
  • Make eye contact with your partner.
  • Explore broadly first; decide in the final 40 seconds.

Part 3 (10 mins)

  • Frame, develop two or three signposted angles, land it.
  • Listen intently during your partner's 2 minutes.
  • Qualify or extend in the 45-second follow-up.

Recovering Naturally Under Pressure

  • "Sorry, would you mind repeating the question?" (Zero penalty.)
  • "Let me put that slightly more precisely..." (Self-correction shows active control.)
  • "That is not an angle I have weighed up before, though..." (Buys thinking time naturally.)
