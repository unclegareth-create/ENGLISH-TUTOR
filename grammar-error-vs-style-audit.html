Error or Just Style? Why Over-Correcting (and Automated Checkers) Hurt Good Exam Essays

Nothing ruins a strong B2 or C1 writer faster than a red pen that refuses to distinguish between a broken grammar rule and a tighter stylistic preference. In nineteen years of marking Cambridge and IELTS scripts (fifteen of them lecturing at Belfast Metropolitan College), I have watched confident candidates tie themselves in knots trying to appease automated grammar checkers and heavy-handed essay rewrites.

When you tell a student that a completely grammatical clause like "discussed if", "most of the species", or "pose a challenge to" is an error, two bad things happen right off the bat: you falsely crush their Error-Free Sentence Ratio (EFSR), and you scare them into stuffing unnatural thesaurus words (plethora, paramount, ubiquitous) into every paragraph.

Finding 01 · What Classroom Data Shows When You Separate Style From Error

How Over-Correcting Slashes a Band 7.0 Essay from 60.0% to 33.3% EFSR

Cambridge C1/C2 and IELTS examiners mark Grammatical Range & Accuracy separately from Lexical Resource and Register. Mixing the two up distorts every diagnostic number on the page.

98 Portal Rows: 62 Errors (63.3%)
vs 36 Clean Style Upgrades (36.7%)

Over One-Third of Useful Upgrades Start From 100% Clean English

When I audited 98 logged sentence upgrades across ten active 1-to-1 portals, 62 rows (63.3%) fixed genuine grammatical or lexical mistakes (such as "discuss about" or "countless damages"). The remaining 36 rows (36.7%) started from sentences that were already 100% grammatical — upgraded purely to teach a sharper C1 cleft sentence, a participle clause, or a natural British idiom (such as turning "were a bit disappointing" into "were rather underwhelming").

In essay marking, failing to draw a bright line between those two buckets causes real harm. Take Case Study A in my audited dataset: a 15-sentence IELTS Academic Task 2 essay on global biodiversity loss. Nine of its fifteen sentences (60.0% EFSR) contain zero grammatical errors, comfortably meeting the Band 7.0 baseline for "frequent error-free sentences".

Yet four of those nine clean sentences contain phrasing that automated writing tools and over-zealous tutors routinely slash in red: "discussed if" (instead of "debated whether"), "most of the living species", "most of the species", and a wordy "there has not been..." passive. Miscounting those four stylistic notes as grammar errors crashes the student's reported EFSR from 60.0% (9/15) down to 33.3% (5/15) — a 26.7 percentage-point penalty without a single broken grammar rule! Across all 45 audited exam sentences in three scripts, over-correction artificially drags pooled EFSR down by 20.0 percentage points (from 51.1% to 31.1%).

Finding 02 · Automated Checkers vs Human Examiners

Crying Wolf on Passive Frames While Missing Real L1 Traps

Most commercial automated grammar checkers were built to trim American business emails, not mark British academic essays. That design mismatch creates two dangerous blind spots for exam candidates.

Blind Spot A · Crying Wolf on Clean English

Attacking C1 Passives & Grammatical Clauses

Automated writing tools routinely underline "It is widely argued that..." or "It is strongly recommended that bus lanes be widened" as "weak passive voice". In IELTS Task 2 and Cambridge C1 proposals, impersonal passive reporting frames are high-scoring academic register! Even worse, AI rewriters talk students into replacing clean phrases like "a very important role" with clunky dictionary curiosities.

Blueprint: It + BE + adverb + V3 + that + clause [Keep clean!]
Student Original: "It is widely argued that public museums play a very important role in civic education."
Examiner Verdict: 100% Error-Free Sentence! Keep the passive reporting frame intact; at most, tighten very important to vital as an optional style note — never swap it for bloated thesaurus words like paramount.
Blind Spot B · Sailing Past Real L1 Errors

Missing Legal-Plural & False-Friend Traps

While automated checkers fuss over "discussed if", they sail right past genuine L1 errors whenever the wrong word happens to be a valid English spelling in another context. Because "damages" is a legal noun (court compensation), "researches" is a third-person verb, and "job perspectives" is syntactically tidy, basic checkers leave them unmarked — and human examiners penalise every single one.

Blueprint: widespread damage [uncountable harm]  ≠  damages [court money]
Student Original: "Unregulated trawling has caused countless damages to fragile seabed habitats."
Examiner Repair: "Unregulated trawling has caused widespread damage to fragile seabed habitats." (Genuine uncountable-noun error.)
