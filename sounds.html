IELTS Speaking Part 2 · Cue Card

Describe a conversation or speech you heard that left a lasting impression on you.

You should say:

  • 1. Context: who was speaking and where you heard them
  • 2. Core Topic: what the discussion or speech was mainly about
  • 3. Delivery: how the speaker used their voice, pace, or emphasis
  • 4. Reflection: and explain why their words stayed with you afterwards.

Target Prosody Challenge: Include at least one Wh-cleft (What struck me most was...) and pause for half a beat before `was`.

Gareth's Practical Structuring Blueprint

Three Rules Before You Speak

1. The 1-Minute Note Secret

Write only 4 single trigger keywords down the margin (one per prompt bullet). Never write full sentences—reading notes flattens your natural intonation immediately.

2. The 3-Act Story Arc

Past Context (30s) → Sensory Snapshot / Core Scene (45s) → Reflection / 'So What?' (45s). Let your pitch drop calmly on your final reflection.

3. The Cambridge Anchor

The four bullets are springboards, not an interrogation checklist. If you finish the bullets at 1 minute 20 seconds, deepen your reflection and comparison until the examiner halts you.

10-Item Stress-Shift & Connected Speech Workout

ITEM 01 / 10Intrusive /r/ Liaison

In rapid British academic delivery of “The idea of it is that law and order must go on”, where does an intrusive /r/ naturally appear?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
Non-high vowels (/ə/, /ɔː/, /ɑː/) followed immediately by an initial vowel trigger an intrusive /r/ glide: /aɪˈdɪə.r‿əv/ and /ˈlɔː.r‿ənd/. By contrast, rounded /əʊ/ in “go on” triggers a labial-velar /w/ glide (/ˈɡəʊ.w‿ɒn/).
ITEM 02 / 10Bilabial /w/ Liaison

Which connected glide links the two vowels in “must go on” (/ˈɡəʊ ɒn/) in natural British speech?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
Because the diphthong /əʊ/ ends with rounded lips, moving directly into the vowel of “on” creates a natural bilabial glide: /ˈɡəʊ.w‿ɒn/.
ITEM 03 / 10Alveolar Plosive Elision

In the clause “postpone the next meeting”, why do native speakers pronounce “next meeting” as /ˈneks ˈmiː.tɪŋ/?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
In standard C1/C2 connected speech, medial /t/ and /d/ routinely elide between consonant clusters (pos(t)pone, nex(t) meeting) so the speaker preserves stress-timed rhythm without robotic consonant release.
ITEM 04 / 10Wh-Cleft Nuclear Stress

In the Wh-cleft “What really unravels a weak paragraph is the lack of syntactic control”, how should your pitch behave?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
A Wh-cleft packages known background inside the opening nominal clause (marked with a ↘↗ fall-rise on paragraph) and reserves primary [ˈnuː.klɪə stres] for the new complement after is: sɪnˈtæk.tɪk kənˈtrəʊl ↘.
ITEM 05 / 10Negative Inversion Tonic Stress

When you open a sentence with “Seldom have examiners witnessed…”, how do you signal the inversion vocally?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
Fronted negative adverbials (Seldom, Rarely, Not only, Under no circumstances) exist for rhetorical emphasis. Give the fronted adverbial high tonic weight (/ˈsel.dəm ⬆↘/) so the inverted auxiliary /həv/ flows naturally behind it.
ITEM 06 / 10Participle Clause Boundary Rhythm

In “Having dismantled the template, the candidate wrote with genuine authority”, what happens at the comma after “template”?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
Fronted participle clauses are unfinished syntactic frames. Holding a continuation rise (ˈtem.pleɪt ↗ |) tells the examiner’s ear that the main subject (the candidate) is about to resolve the thought.
ITEM 07 / 10C1 Stress Shift · RECORD

Choose the accurate British IPA contrast between “a detailed record” (noun) and “to record the data” (verb):

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
Two-syllable noun/verb pairs place primary stress on the first syllable for the noun (/ˈrek.ɔːd/) and shift stress to the second syllable for the verb while reducing the first vowel to /ɪ/ or /ə/ (/rɪˈkɔːd/).
ITEM 08 / 10C1 Stress Shift · ATTRIBUTE

How does the vowel in the first syllable change between the noun “a leadership attribute” and the verb “to attribute success to hard work”?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
In British academic English, shifting primary stress onto the second syllable of the verb (/əˈtrɪb.juːt/) automatically collapses the unstressed initial vowel into a crisp schwa /ə/.
ITEM 09 / 10C1 Stress Shift · PROJECT

In the sentence “This research project is designed to project long-term trends”, which stress pattern is correct?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
Noun: /ˈprɒdʒ.ekt/ (short /ɒ/ on stressed syllable 1). Verb: /prəˈdʒekt/ (schwa /ə/ on syllable 1, nuclear stress on syllable 2).
ITEM 10 / 10Weak Form Reduction in C1 Clauses

In “the lack of syntactic control”, how are the function words “the” and “of” pronounced in natural British prosody?

Cambridge C1 Phonetics & Prosody Model:
Reducing unstressed prepositions and articles to /ə/ (/ðə læk əv/) creates the rhythmic contrast that makes your C1 lexical collocations (sɪnˈtæk.tɪk kənˈtrəʊl) stand out effortlessly.
