Four-Genre Register & Tone Workbench

In Cambridge C2 Proficiency Writing Part 2, you have 45 minutes to write 280 to 320 words in one chosen genre: a Report, a Review, an Article, or a Formal Letter. In nineteen years of marking Cambridge scripts, I have seen spotless grammar and rich C2 lexis lose two full marks on Communicative Achievement (dropping from 5 down to 3) for one avoidable fault: register bleed.

What is register bleed? It is letting the voice of one publication leak into another: writing a committee report with magazine hype, stuffing a cultural review with council legalese, or firing off casual slang in a formal letter to the editor.

Civic Museum Evening Access Scheme: Interim Findings

Purpose

This report evaluates the sixteen-week evening pay-what-you-can pilot at the Civic Museum and outlines practical adjustments ahead of next quarter's budget review.

Attendance and Revenue Impact

Evening footfall exceeded daytime averages by roughly thirty-eight per cent, with voluntary donations offsetting nearly half of the supplementary invigilation bill. Crucially, visitor surveys indicate that the flexible tariff broadened local participation among shift workers and students rather than cannibalising weekend ticket sales. Should the scheme be retained, modest staggered staffing allowances are advised to relieve pressure on Friday rosters.

Key Traps to Avoid in a CPE Report

  • Emotional cheerleading: adjectives like amazing, magical, or game-changer immediately shatter committee neutrality.
  • First-person storytelling: avoid narrating your personal museum visit; anchor claims in surveys, tallies, and observation.
  • Vague recommendations: close with concrete, realistic actions linked directly to the operational issues you identified.
Cultural Review Reader: Arts & Architecture Quarterly

Quiet Revolution After Dusk: Inside the Civic Museum's Late Openings

Lowering the financial drawbridge after six o'clock has achieved something subtle inside the Civic Museum: it strips the hushed solemnity from the sculpture court without cheapening the work. Under softer evening lighting, the nineteenth-century bronzes carry a warmth rarely noticeable at midday, and the mix of unhurried locals gives the rooms genuine pulse.

Where the experiment falters is acoustic discipline. Clatter from the atrium bar spills awkwardly into the watercolour wing, breaking concentration beside the smaller studies. Even with that flaw, this remains an assured, generous cultural initiative well worth a Thursday detour.

Key Traps to Avoid in a CPE Review

  • Bureaucratic stiffness: phrasing like operational deficit or satisfactory compliance turns arts criticism into a safety audit.
  • Empty star-rating hype: saying something was super enjoyable or five stars tells a C2 reader nothing; pinpoint exact details.
  • All summary, no verdict: describe only enough context to ground your judgement, then weigh strengths against real flaws.

Seven O'Clock and No Price Tag: How an Empty Gallery Learned to Breathe

Nobody is hurrying to squeeze twenty pounds' worth of culture out of an afternoon pass. A student leans against a radiator sketching marble drapery; two hospital porters debate a seascape before heading home.

Ask yourself when you last stepped inside a warm public building after dark without being nudged to buy something. Dropping the fixed ticket price looks like a minor accounting experiment, yet it exposes a much deeper urban craving for spaces held genuinely in common.

Key Traps to Avoid in a CPE Article

  • Essay-style throat clearing: never open with "The purpose of this article is to discuss..."; hook the reader on line one.
  • Subheading overload: avoid stiff numbered report sections unless the magazine style clearly warrants a light subhead.
  • Chatty blog slang: warm and lively does not mean sloppy; keep sentence rhythm tight and imagery freshly observed.
Formal Letter to the Editor Reader: National Broadsheet Letters Page

Dear Editor,

I am writing in response to Tuesday's leader questioning whether the Civic Museum can afford to sustain its evening pay-what-you-can openings. While the trustees are entirely right to note that extended security rosters carry real costs, judging a public gallery solely on turnstile receipts misunderstands why municipalities fund museums in the first place.

Rather than curtailing late access prematurely, the council should publish a transparent twelve-month review weighing evening attendance gains against modest commercial sponsorship. Public trust, once earned, is far costlier to rebuild than an extra shift allowance.

Yours faithfully,
E. Henderson

Key Traps to Avoid in a CPE Formal Letter

  • Contractions and slang: don't, it's, or no-brainer instantly undercut broadsheet authority.
  • Ranting without concession: acknowledging the opposing budget concern makes your policy push far harder to dismiss.
  • Mismatched sign-off: use Yours faithfully with Dear Editor / Sir or Madam, and Yours sincerely only when addressing a named person.

CPE Part 2 Report Brief Verified: 300 words (Band 5/5)

Your city's Municipal Cultural Committee piloted a sixteen-week evening pay-what-you-can scheme at the Civic Museum. Write a report evaluating (1) how the trial affected visitor diversity, (2) the chief operational pressures observed, and (3) practical adjustments before making the scheme permanent.

Evening Pay-What-You-Can Access at the Civic Museum: Trial Assessment

Introduction

This report assesses the sixteen-week evening access trial conducted at the Civic Museum on Thursdays and Fridays between 18:00 and 21:00. Drawing on turnstile tallies, staff debriefings, and visitor questionnaires, it evaluates changes in audience reach, outlines operational pressures, and proposes targeted adjustments ahead of a permanent funding decision.

Audience Diversity and Uptake

Attendance across the trial exceeded daytime equivalents by nearly forty per cent, with voluntary contributions averaging just under half the standard admission fee. More notably, demographic surveys indicate that the flexible tariff achieved its core civic aim. Shift workers, postgraduate students, and residents from lower-income outer wards accounted for almost two-thirds of evening entries, a marked departure from the predominantly tourist daytime profile. Repeat visits also rose steadily, suggesting that removing a fixed price barrier encourages casual, unhurried engagement rather than dutiful one-off sightseeing.

Operational Pressures

Nevertheless, three recurring operational strains emerged during the review period. First, invigilation rosters proved difficult to sustain, as gallery attendants frequently faced late finishes followed by early weekend openings. Second, unscheduled group arrivals occasionally caused congestion in the narrower sculpture rooms, raising genuine conservation concerns around humidity and safe viewing distances. Third, acoustic spillover from the atrium cafe distracted visitors seeking quieter contemplation in adjacent portrait galleries.

Recommendations

Should the committee elect to retain evening access on a permanent footing, three proportionate measures are strongly advised:

  1. Introduce staggered shift allowances so evening duty rotates fairly without eroding daytime staffing resilience.
  2. Implement free timed-entry reservations for parties exceeding six people, thereby preserving open access while regulating gallery density.
  3. Install discreet acoustic panelling between the atrium and adjoining rooms to safeguard quiet viewing conditions.

With these safeguards in place, the scheme represents a financially viable and socially worthwhile extension of public cultural provision.

CPE Part 2 Article Brief Verified: 295 words (Band 5/5)

An international arts magazine is running a series on how public cultural spaces are reinventing themselves. Write an article (1) describing how evening pay-what-you-can access changes a museum's atmosphere, (2) exploring how it challenges traditional attitudes towards high culture, and (3) reflecting on what it reveals about civic life today.

After Six O'Clock: When a Museum Drops Its Price Tag

Step beneath the vaulted stonework of the Civic Museum at seven on a wet Thursday evening, and the familiar choreography of gallery-going quietly dissolves. Gone is the brisk weekend procession of visitors trying to squeeze value from a steep admission ticket. In its place is a far looser gathering: nurses arriving off shift in trainers, teenagers sketching beside Roman busts, and retired neighbours pausing for twenty minutes before catching the bus home.

Removing a fixed entrance fee does something subtler than widen attendance figures; it dismantles an unspoken hierarchy. For generations, imposing facades and hefty ticket prices subtly signalled that serious art belonged to those with spare afternoons and disposable income. When visitors are invited to leave whatever coin or note feels reasonable, that quiet threshold anxiety recedes. You no longer feel obliged to perform awe or march dutifully past every plinth to justify the outing. Paradoxically, giving people permission to stay briefly often persuades them to linger far longer.

What strikes you most, however, is how sharply these evenings expose a wider urban hunger. Modern cities overflow with places to spend money and bright screens to stare into, yet genuinely shared indoor spaces where nobody is nudged to buy a second coffee have grown remarkably scarce. Watching strangers trade hushed remarks over a watercolour at dusk reminds you that a civic institution is not merely a solemn vault for treasured objects; it is a living room held in common.

Cynics will rightly ask who pays the electricity bill when municipal budgets tighten. Still, if a city measures its cultural vitality solely by turnstile receipts, it misses the quieter dividend visible every Thursday night: a public that actually feels at home among its own inheritance.

Content

5 / 5

Covers all three sub-questions seamlessly without mechanical lists: lived evening atmosphere (Para 1), dismantling cultural exclusivity (Para 2), and the wider urban appetite for non-commercial shared space (Para 3 to 4).

5 / 5

Reads like a genuine broadsheet weekend feature. Direct reader invitation ("Step beneath...", "You no longer feel obliged...") blends warmth with crisp intellectual authority.

Organisation

Language
