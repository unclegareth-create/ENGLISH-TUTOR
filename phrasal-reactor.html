The Premise: Cybernetic Pressure Meets Real English

Phrasal verbs are universally regarded as one of the hardest parts of learning English. Unlike single-word verbs, a phrasal verb changes its meaning completely depending on which small particle (up, off, down, out, across) follows it. When learners memorise them from dry alphabetical tables, their brains freeze during spontaneous conversation.

Phrasal Reactor gamifies this process by putting you into a high-stakes emergency simulator. Under a ticking timer, you can't afford to translate through your native language – you have to rely on instinct and rapid semantic pattern recognition.

How to Play: Controls & Rules

1

Read the Target Definition

At the top of the console, a clear definition appears (e.g. “To invent a story or excuse”).

2

Check the Base Verb Core

The pulsing core shows the base root verb (e.g. MAKE, PUT, COME).

3

Inject the Correct Particle

Click one of the four candidate particles before the reactor bar depletes. For MAKE + “invent a story”, inject UP!

4

Survive 10 Consecutive Cores

Stabilise all 10 reactor cores to prevent system meltdown and claim your final mission debrief.

What You'll Learn

  • High-frequency B2 & C1 verbs: Master crucial verbs like PUT UP WITH (tolerate), COME ACROSS (encounter by chance), CALL OFF (cancel), and TURN DOWN (reject an offer).
  • Disentangling confusing particle pairs: Stop mixing up PUT OFF (postpone) with PUT ON (wear / stage) or PUT UP WITH (endure).
  • Reflexive speed under exam conditions: Build the subconscious processing speed required for Cambridge Use of English Parts 1, 2, and 4, and IELTS Speaking fluency.

A Note from Gareth: Why Gamified Practice Works

When you sit with a grammar textbook, you have infinite time to hesitate, double-guess yourself, and translate. But real conversations move at 150 words per minute. Phrasal Reactor bridges that gap: by introducing a ticking visual clock, it forces your brain to create direct neural associations between the meaning and the particle. Two minutes a day will do more for your phrasal verb fluency than hours of passive staring.
