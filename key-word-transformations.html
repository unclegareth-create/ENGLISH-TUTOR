Cambridge Use of English · Part 4

Key Word Transformations: 6 Essential Patterns & Practice (B2–C2)

Part 4 of the Cambridge Reading & Use of English paper – Key Word Transformations – is notoriously unforgiving. But it isn't random. Cambridge examiners recycle specific structural patterns year after year. Once you learn to recognise these formulas, you can reliably capture full marks.

The Rules of the Game

Before looking at the grammar formulas, keep these immutable exam mechanics in mind:

  • Word counts: In B2 First, use 2 to 5 words. In C1 Advanced and C2 Proficiency, use 3 to 6 words. Any response exceeding the word limit automatically scores zero.
  • Contractions: Contractions count as two words (e.g. didn't = did not = 2 words; can't = can not = 2 words).
  • The Keyword: You must never alter the keyword in any way (never change singular to plural, capitalize, or alter tense).
  • Split Marks: Each question is worth up to two marks. The official mark scheme divides the answer into two independent halves (e.g., part 1 | part 2). Even if you make a mistake in one half, an accurate other half still awards you 1 mark!

The 4-Step Elimination & Deconstruction Strategy

Never guess a transformation by ear. At my desk, I teach every candidate to break Part 4 down in four steps before writing a single word:

  1. Step 1 — Strike Out the Echoes: Cross out every word from Sentence 1 that already appears in Sentence 2. This isolates the exact missing meaning you have to rebuild.
  2. Step 2 — Inspect Tense Harmony: Check the verb tense of Sentence 1 (past simple, present perfect, modal past). Your gap must preserve that exact timeframe.
  3. Step 3 — Check Voice Shift: Has the object of Sentence 1 moved to the front of Sentence 2? If so, you need an active-to-passive shift (or a nominalised noun phrase).
  4. Step 4 — Solve the Collocation or Pattern: Fit the remaining meaning into the C1 grammatical frame or dependent preposition required by the unchanged keyword.

The 6 Recurring Examination Patterns

1 Passive Reporting Verbs

Shifting from “People claim / rumour that...” into “Subject + is said / thought / alleged + to have [past participle]”.

Sentence 1: Police believe that the suspect has fled the country. [BELIEVED]

Transformation: The suspect is believed to have fled the country.

2 Conditionals & Inverted Structures

Replacing standard conditionals (“If it hadn't been for...”) with negative inversion (“Had it not been for...” or “Were it not for...”).

Sentence 1: Without your timely advice, we would have failed. [HAD]

Transformation: Had it not been for your timely advice, we would have failed.

3 Verb Complementation & Dependent Prepositions

Switching direct quotes to reporting verbs requiring prepositions and gerunds (e.g. accuse of, apologize for, deny, prevent from).

Sentence 1: “I'm sorry I arrived so late,” said Mark. [APOLOGISED]

Transformation: Mark apologised for having arrived / for arriving so late.

4 Fixed Idiomatic Collocations

Transforming common verbs into fixed nominal or prepositional idioms: came as a surprise to, in the mood for, have no intention of, take for granted.

Sentence 1: The cancellation of the flight completely surprised us. [CAME]

Transformation: The cancellation of the flight came as a complete surprise to us.

5 Unreal Past, Wishes & Preferences

Using structures like “would rather you [past simple]”, “it is high time we [past simple]”, or “wish I had [past participle]”.

Sentence 1: We really ought to make a final decision now. [TIME]

Transformation: It is high time we made a final decision.

6 Degree, Comparison & Result

Flipping too... to into not... enough, or converting simple comparatives into nowhere near as... as or by far the most.

Sentence 1: The second exam was much harder than the first. [NEAR]

Transformation: The first exam was nowhere near as hard as the second.

Exam Practice: 6 Interactive Questions

Test yourself on these genuine C1/C2 exam-style challenges. Try completing each gap on paper first (using 3 to 6 words), then expand the box to check the exact mark breakdown:

1. She didn't pay any attention to the instructions. [NOTICE] Reveal answer

She ______ the instructions.

Answer: took no notice of / didn't take any notice of

Mark 1: took no notice · Mark 2: of (4 words)

2. I'd prefer you not to smoke in the kitchen. [RATHER] Reveal answer

I ______ in the kitchen.

Answer: would rather you did not smoke / would rather you didn't smoke

Mark 1: would rather you · Mark 2: did not smoke (6 words)

3. They think the ancient temple was destroyed by an earthquake. [THOUGHT] Reveal answer

The ancient temple ______ by an earthquake.

Answer: is thought to have been destroyed

Mark 1: is thought · Mark 2: to have been destroyed (6 words)

4. We could not go swimming because of the heavy thunderstorm. [PREVENTED] Reveal answer

The heavy thunderstorm ______ swimming.

Answer: prevented us from going

Mark 1: prevented us · Mark 2: from going (4 words)

5. If the coastguard had not intervened swiftly, the yacht would have sunk. [HAD] Reveal answer

______ the coastguard's swift intervention, the yacht would have sunk.

Answer: Had it not been for

Mark 1: Had it not · Mark 2: been for (5 words)

6. The film adaptation was much less convincing than the original novel. [NEAR] Reveal answer

The film adaptation was ______ convincing as the original novel.

Answer: nowhere near as

Mark 1: nowhere near · Mark 2: as (3 words)

The 5-Point Proofreading Checklist

  1. Count the words: Did you stay strictly within the 3–6 word limit? Remember that contractions count as 2.
  2. Keyword unchanged: Did you accidentally tweak the keyword (e.g. wrote preventing instead of PREVENTED)?
  3. Grammatical concord: Does the subject agree with the verb? Is the tense aligned with the original sentence?
  4. Identical semantic meaning: Does the second sentence communicate the exact same nuance without adding extra unsupported ideas?
  5. Spelling precision: Check every letter. Cambridge mark schemes do not forgive spelling mistakes on Part 4.
