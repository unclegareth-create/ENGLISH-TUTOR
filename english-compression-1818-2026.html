200 Years of Packing Ideas Into Fewer Words: How English Moved From Victorian Clauses to Modern C1 Academic Prose

In nineteen years of marking exam essays (fifteen of them lecturing at Belfast Metropolitan College), the single biggest misconception I see at B2 and C1 is the belief that "advanced academic English" means forty-word sentences tied in knots with which, although, semicolons, and moreover.

To show why those sprawling sentences unravel on exam day, I measured 36 texts (5,296 words) across two centuries: 15 classic 19th-century passages from Frankenstein (1818) to Baskervilles (1902), their 15 paired 2026 C1/C2 academic transfers, and 6 full-length 2026 C1/C2 essays. Modern formal English did not get longer—it got 30.2% shorter and five times denser.

Why Rambling 38-Word Sentences Lose Marks Today

In 1886, Robert Louis Stevenson could write a majestic fifty-word sentence in Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde stitched together with semicolons, dashes, which, and in proportion as. Readers sat by a coal fire with an entire evening to spare; they enjoyed watching a sentence wind its way down half a page.

Try writing like that in a timed IELTS Task 2 or Cambridge C1 Advanced essay today and two things go wrong right off the bat. First, by word twenty-eight you lose track of your main subject and verb agreement. Second, modern academic English simply does not build complexity that way any more.

As corpus linguists Douglas Biber and Bethany Gray famously noted in university research prose—and as my 15 paired literary-to-academic units measure word for word—modern formal English underwent a quiet revolution between 1818 and 2026:

  • Mean sentence length shrank by 30.2%: dropping from 24.97 words in the 19th-century passages to a crisp 17.42 words in their paired 2026 C1/C2 academic transfers (p = 0.0020).
  • Semicolon and colon chaining collapsed by 83.8%: falling from 1.85 to 0.30 per 100 words, while finite which / although / because subordination per sentence dropped by 52.6% (1.32 → 0.63).
  • Yet ideas per sentence stayed constant (2.70 → 2.92, p = 0.478): modern C1 prose packs the exact same number of propositions into 30% fewer words because nominalisations and participle clauses surged fivefold (+407.4%, from 1.49 to 7.55 per 100 words).
The Golden Rule for Exam Day

High-scoring C1/C2 prose is short, dense, and unhurried. Instead of tacking on another ", which means that... because..." clause, turn one verb into a clean noun phrase (budget cuts, staff retention) and attach the result with an -ing or -ed participle.

🔬 For Fellow Teachers & Historical Linguists: Exact Equations & Paired t-Test Tables (N = 36, 5,296 Words) ▼ Expand Details

For each text T with W words and S sentences (mean length L̄ = W / S), Finite Subordination & Periodic Depth is FSDI = (100 / W) · (C_sub + C_punct), where C_sub counts closed-class finite subordinators/relatives (that, which, who, whom, whose, where, when, while, although, though, because, since, as, if, unless, until, whereas, whereby) and C_punct counts periodic chaining marks (;, :, dashes). Nominal & Participial Compression is NPCI = (100 / W) · (N_nom + N_part), counting abstract derivational nominalisations (≥ 7 characters in -tion, -sion, -ment, -ance, -ence, -ity, -ness, -ism, -ship, -cy) and non-finite participial clauses / prepositional gerunds. Total propositions per sentence is PPS = 1 + FSPS + NPPS = 1 + (L̄ / 100)(FSDI + NPCI), and non-finite compression share is K = NPCI / (FSDI + NPCI).
