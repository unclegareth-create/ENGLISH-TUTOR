True/False/Not Given & Matching Headings Without Word-Match Traps

After 19 years of teaching IELTS, including 15 at Belfast Met, I see the same pattern every week: strong Band 6.5 readers spot an identical noun in the passage, feel a rush of recognition, and stamp TRUE straight into a carefully planted contradiction or silence trap.

My Press-Desk rule is blunt: never stamp a verdict until you can put your finger on the exact sentence that proves or contradicts the claim, or prove that the passage stays silent on the specific link being tested.

Stage 01 · The Trap Map

The Three-Way Verdict Compass

Most lost marks on IELTS Academic Reading come from blurring FALSE (the text disagrees) with NOT GIVEN (the text never settles the question). Use this three-way legal test before touching a single answer box.

TRUE Paraphrased Match

Same Meaning, Fresh Clothing

Every factual piece of the statement agrees with the passage. IELTS almost always changes the wording (verb to noun, active to passive, concrete detail to accurate category), so expect synonyms rather than echoes.

Passage: "Municipal budgets fell short of covering the bridge repairs."
Claim: "Local authorities lacked enough funding to pay for bridge restoration."
FALSE Direct Contradiction

The Text Rules It Out

The passage states the opposite, reverses the comparison, narrows the claim out of existence, or makes the statement factually impossible. You can highlight the exact line that disproves it.

Passage: "Only three coastal districts piloted the tidal barrier."
Claim: "Tidal barriers were trialled across every coastal district."
NOT GIVEN Verified Silence

Familiar Topic, Missing Link

The subject nouns appear right there in the paragraph, yet the writer never confirms or denies the specific claim, comparison, motive, cost, or future outcome. Do not fill the gap with outside knowledge.

Passage: "Engineers tested both glass and ceramic tiles in the tunnel."
Claim: "Ceramic tiles proved more expensive to manufacture than glass."

Four Famous IELTS Lexical Traps

When a statement echoes exact vocabulary from the passage, slow down and check for one of these four switches.

Trap 01 · Quantifier Stretch Usually FALSE

Scope Shift (all vs most, only vs mainly)

The passage uses a cautious quantifier such as several, most, or primarily; the statement silently inflates it into all, exclusively, or never.

Passage: "Most northern ferries switched to hybrid fuel."
Claim: "All northern ferries now run on hybrid fuel."
Trap 02 · Invented Cause Often NOT GIVEN

Causal Swap (led to vs coincided with)

Two trends occur in the same decade, and the question turns simple sequence into cause and effect (caused, forced, resulted from). If the text never links them, it is Not Given.

Passage: "Ticket prices rose in 2019, and bus patronage fell that autumn."
Claim: "Higher fares caused the drop in autumn bus travel."
Trap 03 · Timeline Slip Usually FALSE

Time & Status Shift (planned vs completed)

A policy is proposed, under trial, or abandoned years ago, yet the statement reports it in the present tense as an approved or current reality.

Passage: "Regulators have yet to approve commercial flights."
Claim: "Regulators have already authorised commercial routes."
Trap 04 · Missing Ranking Always NOT GIVEN

Unstated Comparison (faster, cheaper, main)

The passage mentions Item A and Item B separately without ever ranking them. The statement quietly inserts more popular than, cheaper than, or the primary reason.

Passage: "The scheme uses steel and timber spans across the estuary."
Claim: "Timber spans were cheaper to source than steel ones."

A

TopicWhen European capitals first installed gas streetlamps in the early nineteenth century, evening illumination functioned chiefly as a civic display of wealth and order along grand boulevards. Lamplighters patrolled prosperous quarters at dusk, while working docks and outlying alleys remained largely dark until dawn. PivotOnce municipal electric grids expanded after 1900, however, artificial light shed its ceremonial role and became routine industrial infrastructure, extending factory shifts, retail trading hours, and commuter timetables deep into the night.

B

TopicFor most of the twentieth century, astronomers peering from hillside observatories were the loudest critics of urban skyglow, yet ecologists have since uncovered far wider biological fallout below. Continuous night-time illumination scrambles the navigation cues of migrating songbirds and interrupts the feeding rhythms of urban bats. PivotEven more surprisingly, field trials across northern Europe revealed that lit roadside meadows suffered sharp drops in nocturnal moth visits, reducing pollination rates for wild plants even during daylight hours.

C

TopicCity councils welcomed light-emitting diodes (LEDs) in the 2010s as an easy climate victory, slashing municipal electricity bills by half while promising longer fixture lifespans. Some officials also hoped crisp white illumination would curb late-night traffic collisions on ring roads. PivotYet the first generation of affordable LEDs emitted intense short-wavelength blue light that scatters far more widely through the atmosphere than warm sodium lamps, and cheaper running costs tempted many towns to install extra poles, actually increasing overall skyglow.

D

TopicReversing this unintended glare does not require plunging pavements into hazardous blackness. Lighting engineers have demonstrated that full-cutoff hoods, which direct beams strictly downward rather than sideways into windows or upward into clouds, trim wasted spill light by over sixty per cent. PivotPaired with warm amber diodes under 2,700 Kelvin and motion-activated dimming after midnight on quiet residential lanes, towns can preserve safe footpath visibility while restoring natural darkness overhead.

E

TopicConvincing cash-strapped councils to replace functional lamps remains politically awkward when upfront retrofit costs fall on tight local budgets. A handful of sparsely populated Alpine villages have trialled switching off selected rural roads after 1 a.m., though full blackouts remain rare. PivotIncreasingly, however, coastal and upland municipalities are discovering an unexpected fiscal offset: certified dark-sky parks now attract shoulder-season stargazing visitors whose hotel and dining spending helps repay the initial lighting upgrade.

Why the 3 Distractor Headings Fail (Cambridge Autopsy)

  • Heading i (Why brighter municipal lighting failed to cut night-time road accidents): Lifted from a single passing sentence in Paragraph C ("Some officials also hoped crisp white illumination would curb late-night traffic collisions"). The paragraph never measures accident rates; its real pivot is about blue-light scatter and extra poles increasing skyglow.
  • Heading vi (A complete ban on public street lighting across rural Europe): Classic Scope Shift trap inflating one minor sentence in Paragraph E ("A handful of sparsely populated Alpine villages have trialled switching off selected rural roads after 1 a.m.") into a continent-wide ban.
  • Heading vii (How astronomers first measured skyglow from mountain observatories): Opening-clause bait lifted from the first thirteen words of Paragraph B. Astronomers are mentioned only as a brief setup before the word yet pivots to nocturnal wildlife and plant pollination.

The 20-Minute Passage Protocol

Mins 0 to 2

Map the Spine Before Reading Details

Read the headline, subhead, and the first sentence of each paragraph. You only need a mental map of where topics live before touching Question 1.

Mins 2 to 8

Clear Matching Headings First

If a passage includes Matching Headings, solve them while your paragraph map is fresh. Read the topic sentence plus the turning-point pivot (yet, however, increasingly) and ignore single-sentence examples.

Mins 8 to 17

Run a Sequential TFNG Sweep

IELTS True/False/Not Given answers almost always appear in passage order. Anchor the name, date, or technical noun, lock the exact sentence, test contradiction against silence, and stamp decisively.

Mins 17 to 20

Enforce the 90-Second Bailout Rule

Never bleed four minutes hunting for a ghost sentence on a Not Given claim. If you have checked cleanly between the previous and next answer anchors and found silence, mark NOT GIVEN, flag it, and protect your time for Passage 3.

Three Belfast Desk Habits That Save Easy Marks
  • Check the rubric wording every time: never write YES / NO when the paper asks for TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN.
  • On paper-based IELTS, there is no extra transfer time at the end of Reading. Write answers straight onto the sheet as you confirm each set.
  • Treat exact word echoes with suspicion: a genuine TRUE answer nearly always wears fresh grammar or synonyms.
