What You'll Learn in This Lesson

Making Logical Deductions About the Past

1. Logical Certainty (must have + V3 / been V-ing) When the evidence points to one conclusion → "He must have been waiting for someone at the gate."
2. Logical Impossibility (can't / couldn't have + V3) When something is logically impossible (never use mustn't have!) → "It can't have been a coincidence."
3. Strong Possibility (may / might well have + V3) When a theory is very plausible → "Sudden terror might well have triggered his heart attack."

'Footprints.'
'A man's or a woman's?'

Old-Fashioned Words (Just for Reading) 1901 Terms
19th-century countryside and medical terms from Chapters 2–3.
Moor-gate / Wicket-gate [noun] A small wooden pedestrian gate set into a hedge or wall leading out onto the open moor.
Scion [noun, literary] A descendant of a wealthy or noble family ("the last scion of the Baskervilles"). In modern English, write heir or descendant.
Spectral [adjective, literary] Ghostly or phantom-like. Great in a ghost story, rarely needed in formal essays.
Modern C1/C2 Words (Great for Exams) Use in Writing & Speaking
High-value analytical words for evaluating arguments and evidence in C1/C2 and IELTS essays.
Plausible [adjective] / Implausible [adj] Seeming reasonable or probable. Collocations: a plausible explanation, a plausible alternative.
Circumstantial [adjective] Pointing indirectly toward a conclusion without proving it directly. Collocations: based on circumstantial evidence.
Calamity [noun] An event causing sudden and widespread damage or distress; a disaster. Collocations: an economic calamity, avert a calamity.

2. The Grammar Pattern: Past Modals of Deduction

Core • 10 Mins C1/C2 Speculation & Evidence

Like Sherlock Holmes weighing up clues, C1/C2 writers use past modals to show how certain they are about what happened in the past. Watch the difference between must have, can't have, and should have:

1. Strong Positive Deduction: must have + V3

Use must have + past participle (or must have been + -ing) when the evidence makes you 95% sure:

• The ash dropped twice, so Sir Charles must have been waiting for someone at the gate.

2. Negative Deduction: can't / couldn't have + V3

When something in the past is logically impossible, use can't have or couldn't have—NEVER mustn't have!

• ✓ The footprints were twenty yards away, so the hound can't have touched him.
• ✗ The hound mustn't have touched him.

3. Plausible Theory: may / might well have + V3

Adding well after may, might, or could upgrades a weak guess into a very plausible explanation:

• The sudden shock might well have triggered his fatal heart attack.

4. Criticism vs. Deduction: should have + V3

Remember: should have + V3 criticises a mistake (something that didn't happen); it is not for logical deduction:

• Sir Charles should never have walked down the Yew Alley alone at night. (= He did walk there, and it was a mistake.)

Quick Grammar Check (3 Marks):

5. Which sentence correctly expresses that it is logically impossible that Sir Charles walked calmly down the path?
6. Why does Holmes say "He must have been waiting by the gate for ten minutes" instead of "He must have waited..."?
7. What does adding the adverb well do in: "Rising living costs may well have contributed to the drop in birth rates"?
