Adapted for Language Study
"The facts of the death of Sir Charles Baskerville have not been entirely cleared up by the coroner's inquiry. Ostensibly attributed to cardiac exhaustion, his collapse in the Yew Alley betrayed symptoms of acute, paralyzing trepidation. Barrymore the butler testified that no other tracks were visible upon the gravel; yet he cannot have examined the border closely, for some twenty yards from the body I observed fresh impressions. Look at the cigar ash dropped twice beside the wicket-gate: Sir Charles must have been standing there for at least ten minutes. Why? Because he might conceivably have had an appointment with someone whose identity he wished to conceal—until the sighting of a gigantic hound precipitated the final calamity." — Adapted for C1/C2 Syntactic Study from Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, The Hound of the Baskervilles (1901), Chapters 2–3 Curricular Adaptation

Arsenal Words (Active C1/C2 Lexis) High-Yield Production

Plausible [adjective] Seeming reasonable, probable, or worthy of belief. High-yield collocations: a plausible hypothesis, superficially plausible, scarcely plausible.
Circumstantial [adjective] Pointing indirectly toward someone's guilt without conclusively proving it. Collocates: purely circumstantial evidence, circumstantial corroboration.
Trepidation [noun] A feeling of fear or agitation about something that may happen. Collocates: mount with trepidation, view the prospect with considerable trepidation.
Calamity [noun] An event causing great and often sudden damage or distress; a disaster. Collocates: avert an impending calamity, precipitate a financial calamity.

2. Syntactic Architecture: The Epistemic Modality Ladder

C1/C2 Deductive Grammar

At C1/C2 level, candidates must calibrate the exact degree of probability when reconstructing past events or evaluating competing hypotheses. Study how Sherlock Holmes's clue board maps onto the four rungs of the Epistemic Modality Ladder:

⚡ POST OFFICE TELEGRAPHS — FORENSIC DOSSIER #221B-01
ORIGIN: CHARING CROSS & GRIMPEN • RECIPIENT: S. HOLMES ESQ., BAKER STREET
EVIDENTIAL LOG: OPEN
"FACTS ARE CONTRA-INDICATED BY SUPERSTITION STOP EXAMINE THE PHYSICAL ANOMALIES BEFORE FORMULATING YOUR HYPOTHESIS STOP"
Clue I: The Missing Boot Sir Henry's unworn brown boot vanished at the Northumberland Hotel, only to be returned before his old black boot was taken. Deduction: A real hound must be being conditioned via scent—a spectral phantom could not possibly require leather footwear.
Clue II: The Printed Times Cut-Out Warning message assembled from pasted words clipped from yesterday's leading editorial in The Times using nail-scissors. Deduction: The sender must have had access to a high-end club or hotel reading room and might conceivably be an educated woman.
Clue III: The Cigar Ash at the Yew Alley Twice the ash dropped from Sir Charles's cigar beside the moor-gate, while his footprints shifted from flat soles to tiptoe. Deduction: He must have been waiting ten minutes before fleeing in mortal terror.

1. Affirmative Logical Necessity

Use must have + past participle (or must have been + -ing) when physical evidence leaves only one logical conclusion:

• The perpetrator must have clipped the words from yesterday's leader column.
• Sir Charles must have been running at full speed when his heart failed.

2. Negative Logical Impossibility

In formal British English, the negative of deductive must have is cannot have or could not have—NEVER mustn't have:

• ✗ Barrymore must not have noticed the tracks.
• ✓ Barrymore cannot possibly have overlooked such deep impressions.

3. Speculative Hedging & Adverbs

Pair may / might / could + have + past participle with C2 epistemic adverbs (conceivably, well, plausibly) to calibrate probability:

• The bearded spy in the hansom cab might conceivably have bribed the hotel boots.
• He may well have anticipated our surveillance.

4. Evidential Sentence Adverbs

Distinguish between surface illusion (ostensibly, outwardly) and reasoned assumption (presumably, in all probability):

• Ostensibly a routine condolence note, the letter was presumably engineered to lure Sir Charles to the gate.

4. The Deduction Style-Shift Lab: Superstitious Legend vs. Forensic Inference

Register & Epistemic Precision

The 1742 Moorland Legend & Press Sensationalism
"Standing over Hugo Baskerville's body, the revellers saw a foul thing—a great, black beast, shaped like a hound, yet larger than any hound that ever mortal eye has rested upon, with blazing eyes and jaws dripping with hellfire! Now the same phantom fiend has returned to claim Sir Charles's soul!"
221B Baker Street Forensic Hypothesis (C2 Analytical)
"While circumstantial accounts corroborate the presence of an abnormally large canid near the wicket-gate, the hypothesis of supernatural agency is wholly untenable. The perpetrator must have exploited Sir Charles's known cardiac vulnerability and ancestral trepidation to induce fatal syncope without inflicting physical trauma."
