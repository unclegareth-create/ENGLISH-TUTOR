Business English for Meetings: 40 Phrases to Lead, Interrupt & Disagree Politely

Many of the senior engineers, product directors, and consultants I coach have excellent English on paper, yet freeze in live stakeholder meetings. The problem rarely lies in basic grammar—it lies in pragmatic calibration: having the exact softening frame ready under time pressure so you can push back, interrupt, or close a tangent without sounding abrasive.

Complete Reference Cheat Sheet

The 40 Essential Business English Meeting Phrases

Keep this reference open during your calls—or choose three phrases before your next stand-up or board review and commit to using each one aloud.

Opening the meeting

  • Shall we get started?
  • Thanks, everyone, for making the time.
  • The aim of today's meeting is to…
  • By the end of this call, I'd like us to have agreed on…
  • We've got 30 minutes, so let's keep things focused.

Managing the agenda

  • Let's move on to the next item.
  • I'd like to come back to that point later, if that's OK.
  • Can we park that for now and pick it up at the end?
  • I'm conscious of time, so let's try to wrap this point up.
  • That's a bit outside the scope of today's meeting.

Inviting contributions

  • Maria, what's your take on this?
  • We haven't heard from the finance team yet.
  • Does anyone have any concerns before we move on?
  • I'd be interested to hear other perspectives.
  • Is there anything we've missed?

Interrupting politely

Interrupting is often necessary, especially on video calls. The key is to signal it, then keep it short.

  • Sorry to interrupt, but…
  • Could I just come in here?
  • If I could just add something quickly…
  • Sorry, can I just check what you mean by…?
  • (When someone interrupts you) If I could just finish my point…

Disagreeing diplomatically

This is the area where direct translation from other languages causes the most problems. In British and many international business settings, a flat “I disagree” or “That's wrong” can sound much stronger than intended. Softening your disagreement makes people far more willing to listen to it.

  • I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree.
  • I take your point. However, I wonder if…
  • That's one way of looking at it. Another might be…
  • I'm not entirely convinced that…
  • I'd see it slightly differently.
  • Have we considered the risk that…?

Same message, different impact

  • Too direct: “Your plan won't work.”
  • Diplomatic: “I'm not sure the plan will work as it stands. Could we look at the timeline again?”
  • Too direct: “That's too expensive.”
  • Diplomatic: “I'm a little concerned about the cost. Is there any flexibility there?”

Checking understanding

  • Just to make sure I've understood: you're saying that…?
  • Could you expand on that a little?
  • Sorry, the line broke up. Could you repeat the last part?
  • So, in other words…
  • What would that mean in practice?

Summarising and agreeing actions

  • So, to sum up what we've agreed…
  • Let's go over the action points.
  • Who's going to take ownership of this?
  • Can we agree on a deadline for that?
  • I'll send round a summary by the end of the day.

Closing the meeting

  • I think we've covered everything. Thanks, everyone.
  • Let's leave it there for today.
  • Same time next week?
  • Thanks for your input; that was really productive.

How to make these phrases stick

Reading a list won't make these phrases automatic. What works for my clients is this:

  1. Choose three phrases you'd genuinely use in your next meeting, not twenty.
  2. Say them out loud several times before the meeting, in a full sentence about your actual work.
  3. Use each one at least once in the meeting, then note how it went.
  4. Add three more the following week.
