1. Read the Scene & Useful Vocabulary

Core • 10 Mins Baskervilles • Chapters 6–7

Read Dr Watson's description of his first journey onto Dartmoor and his walk beside the deadly Grimpen Mire with the naturalist Jack Stapleton. Notice how Conan Doyle starts sentences with where something is to build suspense before naming what is there:

Original Novel Extract (1901)
"Over the green squares of the fields and the low curve of a wood there rose in the distance a grey, melancholy hill, with a strange jagged summit, dim and vague in the distance, like some fantastic landscape in a dream... Later, Stapleton waved his hand toward the great plain: 'That is the great Grimpen Mire,' said he. 'A false step yonder means death to man or beast. Only yesterday I saw one of the moor-ponies wander into it. He never came out. I saw his head for quite a long time craning out of the bog-hole, but it sucked him down at last.' Even as he spoke, there came out of the heart of the great bog a strange, deep, melancholy cry, which the moor-folk say is the Hound of the Baskervilles calling for its prey." — Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, The Hound of the Baskervilles (1901), Chapters 6–7 Original Text
Old-Fashioned Words (Just for Reading) 1901 Terms
19th-century carriage and rural terms from Chapters 6–7.
Wagonette [noun, historical] A four-wheeled horse-drawn country carriage with two long seats facing each other at the back.
Yonder [adverb/determiner, archaic/dialect] "Over there" ("A false step yonder"). Never used in modern essays.
Cromlech [noun, archaeological] An older term for a prehistoric stone circle or tomb.
Modern C1/C2 Words (Great for Exams) Use in Writing & Speaking
Vivid descriptive words that are also used constantly as metaphors in modern essays and journalism.
Quagmire [noun] 1. Soft boggy ground that you sink into. 2. (Metaphor) A messy, complicated situation that is very hard to get out of: a legal/bureaucratic/political quagmire.
Treacherous [adjective] Dangerous and unpredictable because of hidden hazards. Collocations: treacherous driving conditions, treacherous terrain.
Desolate [adjective] Empty, bleak, and lifeless. Collocations: a desolate landscape, feel utterly desolate.
Melancholy [noun / adjective] A deep, quiet, thoughtful sadness. Collocations: a melancholy atmosphere, a sense of melancholy.

2. The Grammar Pattern: Place Inversion vs. Negative Inversion

Core • 10 Mins C1/C2 Descriptive Control

Be careful not to confuse positive place inversion (which does NOT use did) with negative place inversion (which DOES use an auxiliary verb):

1. Positive Place Inversion (No "did"!)

Start with a place phrase + stood / lay / rose / hung / came + a full noun subject:

• ✓ At the end of the avenue stood the dark towers of Baskerville Hall.
• ✗ At the end of the avenue did stand the dark towers...

2. Negative Place Inversion (Use an Auxiliary!)

If the opening word is negative (like Nowhere), you must use an auxiliary verb (was, could, did):

• Nowhere on the moor was a single house to be seen.
• Nowhere else could you find such a bleak landscape.

3. The Pronoun Rule (He / She / It / They)

Never put the verb before a short pronoun like he, she, it, or they in positive place inversion:

• ✓ Into the fog vanished the escaped convict. (Full noun)
• ✓ Into the fog he vanished. (Pronoun stays before the verb!)

4. Lie / Lay vs. Lay / Laid

For position ("be situated"), the verb is lie → lay (past) → lain. Don't confuse it with laid (which needs an object):

• ✓ Beyond the granite hills lay the treacherous bog. (Past of lie)
