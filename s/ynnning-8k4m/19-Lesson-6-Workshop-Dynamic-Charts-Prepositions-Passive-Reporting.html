Task 1 Chart Taxonomy

The 4 Basic Chart Types in IELTS Academic

Understanding what each chart represents dictates which vocabulary and tense systems you must activate.

1. Line Graph
Continuous Movement

Shows continuous change over time for one or several variables. Ideal for tracking trends: fluctuated, shot up, peaked, dipped.

2. Bar Chart
Discrete Comparisons

Compares discrete categories or time periods (like your Further Education chart). Highlights relative heights and rank orders.

3. Pie Chart
Proportions of a Whole

Shows how 100% of a single entity is divided into segments: comprises, accounts for, makes up, represents.

4. Proportional Bar
Stacked Percentages

Stacked columns where each bar equals 100%, allowing simultaneous comparison of category compositions across time periods.

Key Diagnostic Rule

If the chart shows dates across time (e.g. 1991 to 2001, or 2003 to 2006), it is dynamic → use movement verbs in the Past Simple. If it displays only a single date or survey snapshot, it is static → use static comparisons (higher than, the largest proportion) without inventing historical shifts.

Task 1 Syntactic Architecture

The Movement Vault & Two Golden Sentence Formulas

To achieve Band 7.5+ in Grammatical Range, alternate seamlessly between these two core sentence structures.

Formula 1: Verbal Pattern
[Noun Phrase] + [Verb] + [Adverb]
  • • "The unemployment rate rose rapidly between 2005 and 2006."
  • • "Crude oil prices fell steadily after the Iranian revolution."
  • • "Female full-time participation increased dramatically."
Formula 2: Nominal Pattern
There was / were + [a/an + Adjective + Noun] + in...
  • • "There was a rapid rise in the unemployment rate."
  • • "There was a steady fall in crude oil prices."
  • • "The decade witnessed a dramatic increase in female enrolment."

The Two Sentence Architectures: Verb + Adverb ↔ There was a + Adj + Noun

Architecture 1: Noun phrase (Subject) + Verb + Adverb

"The unemployment rate rose rapidly."

"The cost of living fell dramatically."

Architecture 2: There was/has been a + Adjective + Noun + in

"There was a rapid rise in the unemployment rate."

"There has been a dramatic fall in the cost of living."

Exercise 2: Transform Between the Two Architectures
1. The price of oil rose sharply. → There...
Model: "There was a sharp rise in the price of oil."
2. There is a noticeable increase in temperature from May onwards. → Temperature...
Model: "Temperature increases noticeably from May onwards."
3. The number of people attending the theatre has fallen dramatically. → There...
Model: "There has been a dramatic fall in the number of people attending the theatre."

Model: "DVD sales had risen steadily during the first six months of the year."

Model: "The use of alternative energy sources has grown rapidly."

Model: "There was a significant decrease in the popularity of self-catering holidays during the 1990s."
The #1 Task 1 Trap

Prepositions of Change: 'rose by' vs 'rose to'

Changing a single preposition completely alters the statistical meaning. This was the exact area of correction in your Further Education mark-up.

1. Rose BY [amount] → Size of Change

Describes the margin or difference added to the previous figure:

"Newspaper circulation rose by 20,000."
If circulation started at 40,000, adding 20,000 means the final total is now 60,000.

2. Rose TO [figure] → Final Endpoint

Describes the destination point reached on the vertical axis:

"Newspaper circulation rose to 20,000."
Circulation was at 15,000, and it increased by 5,000 to reach a final total of 20,000.

Time Prepositions Quick Guide

in + Year / Month
"in 1970", "in October 2004"
For a single static reading on the axis.
by + Year (Endpoint)
"reached 260,000 by 1990"
For cumulative progress before a final date.
reach (NO Preposition)
"reached a peak of $50"
Never write "reached to a peak".

Task 2 Persuasive Architecture

Matching Argument with Counter-Argument

Rather than dividing your essay into separate "for" and "against" silos, matching an argument with a counter-argument in the same paragraph creates a far more sophisticated, nuanced academic impression.

Sample C1 Paragraph Analysis (Nuclear Energy Debate) Band 8 Balanced Texture

"The claims made by those in favour of nuclear power do not stand up to close examination. It is often said, for instance, that nuclear power does not damage the environment. While it is true that greenhouse gases are not emitted during generation, the mining and refining of uranium causes severe terrestrial contamination. Moreover, many commercial reactors rely on adjacent rivers for cooling, causing thermal pollution that harms aquatic ecosystems. It is also argued that modern reactor safety features make meltdowns virtually impossible. Despite these assurances, catastrophic accidents have occurred—most notably at Chernobyl and Fukushima—proving that no engineering can completely eliminate human error."

Why this scores high:

You concede the valid point first ("While it is true that..."), then immediately puncture it with concrete evidence ("the mining... causes severe contamination").

Examiner Impact:

Shows mature critical thinking rather than naive one-sided advocacy, fulfilling Band 8 Task Response criteria.

C1 Depersonalisation Engine

Impersonal Passive Reporting Verbs

Replace conversational phrases ("People say that...", "Everyone knows that...") with objective academic passives.

Pattern 1: Preparatory 'It' + Passive + That
It is + [said / argued / predicted / widely believed] + that...
  • People say that wind power is harmless. → "It is said that wind power turbines are completely harmless."
  • Experts predict that China will lead. → "It is predicted that China will emerge as the leading economic power."
  • Everyone knows that heart disease kills. → "It is widely known that cardiovascular disease is the primary cause of mortality."
Pattern 2: Subject + Passive + To-Infinitive / As
[Subject] + is/are + [believed / regarded as / considered]...
  • Many people regard the Internet as a force for democracy. → "The Internet is widely regarded as a powerful force for free speech."
  • Researchers hope they will find a cure. → "A viable vaccine is hoped to be discovered in the near future."
  • People believe that computers engage children. → "Computers are believed to be engaging educational tools."

Band 7.5 Range Criteria
Examiner Diagnostic: Weather Station Temperature Extract

"The weather station recorded temperatures of about 18 degrees in 2006. From 2006 to 2008 the temperature increased (rapidly) to 27°C. However, from 2008, the temperature declined (gradually) to around 22°C in 2011. From 2011 to 2017 it fluctuated (slightly), and then rose (dramatically) again. The temperature peaked at just under 35 degrees in 2020."

Strengths: Accurate vocabulary, correct trend reporting, specific data given to support points (27°C, 22°C, 35°C).
Critical Weakness (Capping at Band 6.0): Severe grammatical monotony. Past simple repeated 6 times. Every sentence uses the exact same verb + adverb formulation without any passive forms or nominal There was a + adj + noun structures.
Worksheet 1 Exercise 3: 8 High-Yield Structural Range Transformations
1. From 2011 to 2017 the temperature fluctuated slightly.
Model: "...there was a slight fluctuation in the temperature."
2. The weather station recorded temperatures of 18 degrees in 2006. (Active)
Model (Passive): "Temperatures of 18 degrees were recorded in 2006."
3. There was an enormous fall in the value of the dollar in the last year.
Model: "The value of the dollar fell enormously in the last year."
4. The number of people using public transport rose slightly.
Model: "There was a slight rise in the number of people using public transport."

Model: "The price is predicted to increase dramatically by 2030."

Model (Passive): "Atmospheric pressure is measured by barometers."

Model (Passive): "All components of the computer are checked by the supervisor personally."

Academic Register Guardrails

The 7 Formal Style Rules (British Council Lesson 3)

Examiners immediately deduct marks under Lexical Resource if an essay sounds like a casual chat or an internet forum post.

1. Zero Contractions:

Never write isn’t, don’t, can’t, won’t. Always write full words: is not, do not, cannot.

2. Avoid Multi-Word Phrasal Verbs:

used up our resources → exhausted our resources; bring in laws → enact legislation.

3. Avoid Informal Idioms:

That idea is just a pipe dream → That proposal is simply unfeasible.

4. Never Use Tag Questions:

Nobody wants war, do they? → It is clear that peace remains universal policy.

5. Avoid Conversational Chat:

If you ask me, what we should do is... → In my assessment, policymakers ought to...

6. Avoid Vague Exaggeration:

There are loads and loads of reasons → There are numerous compelling reasons.

7. Strictly Avoid Sarcasm or Irony:

Of course, we all know what big nature-lovers car manufacturers are. → Automobile corporations have historically demonstrated little commitment to environmental conservation.
