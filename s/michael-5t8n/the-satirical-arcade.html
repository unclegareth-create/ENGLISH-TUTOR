The Satirical Arcade: Physical Computing, Automata & The Charm of Imperfection

Exploring Tim Hunkin’s legendary mechanical arcade machines at Southwold Pier and London’s Novelty Automation. What happens when an engineer abandons screens, microcontrollers, and cloud backends to build satirical state machines out of scrap metal, bicycle chains, and pure mechanical timing?

Stage 01 // Warm-Up Discussion

The Analogue Reality Check

Conversational Framing

As software engineers, we spend our lives building intangible, frictionless abstractions: APIs, cloud services, and virtualised runtimes. Before watching the video, discuss these four reality-check prompts:

  • 01
    Tactile Feedback vs. Glass: When was the last time you used a device with purely mechanical feedback (e.g. heavy rotary switches, clacking relays, a mechanical typewriter)? How does physical resistance alter how deliberate your actions feel?
  • 02
    Friction vs. Frictionlessness: Silicon Valley has spent two decades obsessing over "removing all friction" from digital life. Can an interface be too frictionless? What do we lose when every human transaction becomes an effortless thumb tap?
  • 03
    The Catharsis of Breaking Things: When code fails, you get an obscure stack trace or a 500 error. Why is physical destruction or physical comedy (e.g. dropping a heavy steel ball onto a caricature of an oligarch) so much more emotionally satisfying than digital feedback?
  • 04
    The Seaside Tradition: British seaside piers have hosted bizarre, eccentric amusements since the Victorian era (what Brits call "end-of-the-pier novelty"). Why do places by the sea seem to breed such peculiar eccentricities?
Stage 02 // C1 Lexical Toolkit

Mechanics, Satire & Scrap: 8 Essential Expressions

Idioms & Collocations

These authentic idioms and engineering collocations appear throughout the video and discussions of Tim Hunkin’s automata. Notice the blend of engineering precision and dry British irony:

cobbled together (from scrap)

Assembled quickly, roughly, or ingeniously using whatever salvaged materials happen to be lying around.

"His early arcade machines were cobbled together from discarded washing machine motors and bicycle sprockets."
a mechanical contraption

A machine or gadget that appears delightfully intricate, eccentric, or slightly improvised rather than sleekly mass-produced.

"Visitors queue up not for video games, but to crank an eccentric mechanical contraption."
take the mickey (out of someone)

A core British idiom meaning to gently or sharply mock, tease, lampoon, or satirise someone's pretensions.

"The machine unapologetically takes the mickey out of private bankers and corporate tax evaders."
tongue-in-cheek

Humorous, ironic, or subversive; intended to be playful rather than solemn or po-faced.

"His 'Auto-Frisk' machine offers a thoroughly tongue-in-cheek critique of airport surveillance culture."
visceral satisfaction

A deep, physical, almost primal feeling of gratification (e.g. hearing heavy metal clang against wood).

"Tapping an iPad screen simply cannot match the visceral satisfaction of dropping a heavy steel ball."
a Heath Robinson machine

The British equivalent of a Rube Goldberg machine: an absurdly complex contraption built to perform an otherwise trivial task.

"The holiday simulator is a glorious Heath Robinson setup involving hair-dryers and swinging teabags."
planned obsolescence

The deliberate policy of designing products with a limited lifespan so consumers are forced to buy replacements.

"Unlike modern electronics cursed with planned obsolescence, mechanical cams can be repaired indefinitely."
finite-state automaton

A system with discrete operational states governed by explicit inputs; here, mechanical cams and relays forming physical state machines.

"Before microcontrollers existed, these physical automata executed pure deterministic state loops."

Stage 04 // Systems Architecture Debate

The Software Engineer Meets Physical Computing

JetBrains Deep-Dive

Four provocative questions exploring the boundary between legacy software systems (like IDE compilers and cloud runtimes) and physical mechanical state machines:

  • 01
    Deterministic State Machines: Cams vs. Microservices: Tim Hunkin’s machines use rotating camshafts, mechanical relays, and microswitches to sequence actions without a single line of code. In software architecture, we build distributed event-driven systems that frequently fail due to timing races or network partitions. In what ways are mechanical camshafts more robust than microservices—and in what ways are they worse?
  • 02
    Debugging: Grease vs. Stack Traces: When Tim Hunkin debugs an arcade machine, he listens for a squeak, smells a hot motor, or watches a lever catch on a splinter of plywood. When you debug legacy code at JetBrains, you inspect memory dumps, thread pools, and bytecode. Which form of debugging feels more rewarding—and why?
  • 03
    The Ephemerality of Software: Almost every app, framework, and JavaScript build tool written 15 years ago is dead or completely unrunnable today. Yet Hunkin’s mechanical machines have been running in salty seaside air for over 35 years. Why is modern software so hostile to long-term preservation?
  • 04
    Satire as Engineering Therapy: Hunkin uses his engineering skills to take the mickey out of powerful institutions (banks, border police, wellness gurus). If software engineers used code to build subversive, satirical tools rather than productivity boosters, what might that look like?

Sprint: Design a JetBrains Satirical Automaton

2-Minute Pitch
The Brief: Tim Hunkin invites you to build a coin-operated mechanical arcade machine for his London arcade (Novelty Automation), lampooning a modern software engineering nuisance. Rule: Zero microchips, zero screens, zero Wi-Fi. It must operate purely on levers, pulleys, cams, counterweights, and clacking relays.

Choose One Engineering Plague to Lampoon:

1. The Pull Request Tribunal: Senior reviewers rejecting code over variable naming.
2. The Standup Escalator: A meeting that was supposed to take 5 minutes stretching to an hour.
3. The AI Hype Machine: A massive physical contraption that consumes huge power just to print "Hello World".
4. The Legacy Spaghetti Excavator: Pulling one wire that causes an entire ceiling to collapse.
Deliver your 2-minute pitch covering:
  1. What the player sees when they insert their £1 coin.
  2. The physical, mechanical sequence of events (the cams, levers, springs, or ball drops).
  3. The punchline: what the machine physically outputs or does to the player at the end.
Stage 06 // Linguistic Precision

C1 Focus: Inverted Conditionals & Modal Past Deduction

Language Workout

Upgrade the sentences below using Michael's target structures: inverted conditionals (Had + subject + past participle..., Were + subject + to...) and past modal deductions (must have, could hardly have foreseen).

1. If Tim Hunkin had used modern digital screens, his machines would have become obsolete within five years.
HAD

Rewrite using an inverted 3rd conditional without "if":

Had Tim Hunkin used modern digital screens, his machines would have become obsolete within five years.
2. It is almost certain that he spent decades scavenging scrap metal yards across Suffolk.
MUST

Rewrite using a modal past deduction of certainty:

He must have spent decades scavenging scrap metal yards across Suffolk to accumulate such a vast inventory of parts.
3. If software developers were to introduce physical friction into digital workflows, user addiction might drop significantly.
WERE

Rewrite using an inverted 2nd conditional without "if":

Were software developers to introduce physical friction into digital workflows, user addiction might drop significantly.
4. It is impossible that Victorian arcade builders anticipated that their novelty contraptions would outlive 21st-century software.
COULD

Rewrite using could hardly have + past participle:

Victorian arcade builders could hardly have foreseen that their novelty contraptions would outlive 21st-century software.
