Speak First, Think Deeply, Use Natural C1 Language

Six core Cambridge C1 Advanced Part 4 themes. For each topic, pick a question, set the timer for 60–90 seconds, and build an extended answer using 2 or 3 phrases from the topic vocabulary bank.

Part 4 Turn Target (90s)
01:30
1. Take a Nuanced Position Avoid flat “Yes, I agree.” Start with “Up to a point, yes, though...” or “It depends largely on...”
2. Anchor with a Real Example Back up your point in one sentence: a city, an industry, a personal observation, or a contrast between generations.
3. Compare & Widen the Lens Show C1 range by weighing short-term vs long-term impact, or individual choice vs government policy.

1. Technology & Modern Communication

4 Part 4 Prompts
Question 1.1
Has constant connectivity made people better or worse at genuine conversation?
Angle to explore: quantity of messages vs depth of attention.
“On the surface, we're more reachable than ever, yet I'd argue that constant notifications have eroded our ability to stay present in a real conversation.”
Question 1.2
To what extent should governments regulate artificial intelligence in the workplace?
Angle to explore: innovation speed vs worker protection and transparency.
“There's a delicate balance to strike here: heavy-handed regulation could stifle innovation, but leaving it entirely unchecked risks widening inequality.”
Question 1.3
Are digital tools making human memory weaker, or simply freeing it for more creative thinking?
Angle to explore: offloading routine facts vs losing deep recall.
“I tend to see it as a trade-off. We no longer memorise phone numbers, which frees mental space, though relying on search engines can make knowledge feel fairly superficial.”
Question 1.4
Is face-to-face communication becoming a luxury in modern business?
Angle to explore: remote efficiency vs building trust and reading body language.
“For routine updates, video calls are undeniably efficient, but when high-stakes trust is involved, nothing really substitutes for sitting across a table.”

Topic Vocabulary Bank

8 C1 Phrases
strike a delicate balance
Find a fair middle ground between two competing needs.
“Regulators need to strike a delicate balance between safety and innovation.”
stifle innovation
Prevent new ideas or progress from developing.
“Overly strict rules can inadvertently stifle innovation.”
erode attention spans
Gradually weaken people's ability to focus for long periods.
“Short-form feeds have steadily eroded our attention spans.”
double-edged sword
Something that brings both clear benefits and serious drawbacks.
“Remote messaging is a double-edged sword: convenient, yet intrusive.”
screen fatigue
Mental tiredness caused by spending too many hours on devices.
“After back-to-back calls, screen fatigue sets in quickly.”
build rapport
Develop mutual trust and a comfortable connection with someone.
“It's far harder to build rapport over a muted Zoom call.”
data privacy
Control over how personal information is collected and used.
“Consumers are increasingly vocal about data privacy.”
at one's fingertips
Immediately available whenever you need it.
“Having vast archives at our fingertips changes how we learn.”

2. Work, Ambition & Work-Life Balance

4 Part 4 Prompts
Question 2.1
Why do some people define their identity almost entirely through their career?
Angle to explore: social status, sense of purpose, and cultural expectations.
“In many societies, one of the first questions we ask strangers is what they do for a living, so it's hardly surprising that people tie their self-worth to their job title.”
Question 2.2
Is a four-day working week realistic for most industries, or only for office-based roles?
Angle to explore: productivity vs shift work, healthcare, hospitality, and manufacturing.
“While it's proved remarkably effective in knowledge-based sectors, applying it to frontline services like healthcare would require significant extra staffing.”
Question 2.3
Do younger generations value flexibility more than financial security?
Angle to explore: rising living costs vs autonomy over time and location.
“I think flexibility has become a major priority, though that's often a luxury available only once basic financial security is already met.”
Question 2.4
How far is an employer responsible for an employee's mental wellbeing?
Angle to explore: workload design and culture vs personal boundaries.
“An employer can't manage someone's private life, of course, but they do have a clear duty not to normalise chronic overwork.”

Topic Vocabulary Bank

8 C1 Phrases
burnout
Physical and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged overwork.
“Unrealistic deadlines are a fast track to burnout.”
job satisfaction
The sense of fulfilment a person gets from their work.
“Autonomy often matters more for job satisfaction than a small pay rise.”
blur the boundaries
Make the division between two areas (e.g. work and home) unclear.
“Working from home can blur the boundaries between office hours and rest.”
frontline / essential roles
Jobs that must be done in person to keep society running.
“Frontline roles rarely enjoy the flexibility of remote work.”
career progression
Advancing to higher levels of responsibility in one's field.
“Clear paths for career progression help retain talented staff.”
workplace culture
The shared values, habits, and atmosphere inside an organisation.
“A healthy workplace culture encourages people to switch off on time.”
financial security
Having stable, reliable income to cover needs without constant worry.
“Freelancing offers freedom, but less financial security.”
pull one's weight
Do a fair share of the work in a team.
“Resentment builds quickly when one colleague refuses to pull their weight.”

3. Cities, Urban Living & The Environment

4 Part 4 Prompts
Question 3.1
Should private cars be banned from historic city centres altogether?
Angle to explore: air quality and pedestrian life vs accessibility for residents and deliveries.
“In principle, car-free centres transform the atmosphere of a city, provided that reliable public transport and access for elderly or disabled residents are already in place.”
Question 3.2
What makes a city genuinely liveable rather than just economically successful?
Angle to explore: affordable housing, green spaces, safety, and sense of community.
“High salaries mean very little if commuting takes two hours a day and ordinary workers are priced out of living near the centre.”
Question 3.3
Who bears greater responsibility for tackling climate change: individuals or large corporations?
Angle to explore: consumer habits vs industrial scale and legislation.
“Individual choices certainly send a market signal, yet without binding regulations on heavy industry, personal recycling alone won't shift the needle.”
Question 3.4
Do green spaces in cities really change how people behave towards one another?
Angle to explore: stress reduction, shared public life, and social mixing.
“Parks are one of the few places left where people from every background share the same space without having to buy anything.”

Topic Vocabulary Bank

8 C1 Phrases
urban sprawl
The uncontrolled outward growth of a city into surrounding countryside.
“Poor planning has led to endless urban sprawl.”
priced out of the market
Unable to afford living or buying in an area because costs rose too high.
“Young families are being priced out of the housing market.”
public infrastructure
Basic physical systems such as rail, buses, water, and roads.
“You can't restrict cars without investing in public infrastructure first.”
carbon footprint
The total amount of greenhouse gases caused by an activity or person.
“Short-haul flights carry a disproportionately high carbon footprint.”
pedestrianised zone
An area of a town or city closed to motor traffic for walkers.
“Local cafés thrived once the high street became a pedestrianised zone.”
renewable energy
Power generated from natural sources that do not run out (wind, solar).
“Transitioning to renewable energy requires upgrading the power grid.”
quality of life
The general standard of health, comfort, and happiness people experience.
“Clean air and short commutes dramatically improve quality of life.”
shift the needle
Make a noticeable, measurable difference to a situation.
“Small gestures help, but systemic policy is what shifts the needle.”

4. Culture, Media & Tradition

4 Part 4 Prompts
Question 4.1
In a globalised world, is it inevitable that local traditions will gradually disappear?
Angle to explore: commercial uniformity vs renewed pride in regional heritage.
“Some customs inevitably fade when lifestyles change, yet globalisation has also sparked a counter-reaction where people actively preserve local food, music, and languages.”
Question 4.2
Why do people still pay to attend live concerts and theatre when almost everything can be streamed at home?
Angle to explore: shared atmosphere, unpredictability, and the sense of occasion.
“Streaming gives you perfection and convenience, whereas live performance offers a shared, unrepeatable energy that you simply can't replicate on a sofa.”
Question 4.3
Should major museums return historical artefacts to their countries of origin?
Angle to explore: ethical ownership vs global access and conservation capacity.
“It's a sensitive issue, though where objects were taken under colonial rule and the source country has proper facilities, there's a compelling moral case for returning them.”
Question 4.4
Does social media democratise culture, or does it reward superficiality?
Angle to explore: independent creators bypassing gatekeepers vs algorithm-driven outrage.
“It clearly lowers the barrier to entry for talented newcomers, but at the same time, algorithms tend to favour quick emotional reactions over nuance.”

Topic Vocabulary Bank

8 C1 Phrases
cultural heritage
Traditions, monuments, and values passed down through generations.
“Funding local festivals helps keep cultural heritage alive.”
homogenisation
The process of different places or cultures becoming all the same.
“High streets suffer from homogenisation when chains replace local shops.”
a sense of occasion
A special, memorable feeling around an event.
“Dressing up for the theatre creates a real sense of occasion.”
mainstream appeal
Popularity with the general public rather than a niche audience.
“Independent films rarely aim for mainstream appeal.”
gatekeepers
Institutions or people who control access to publishing, media, or funding.
“Musicians no longer need traditional gatekeepers to reach listeners.”
preserve traditions
Protect and maintain customs so they are not lost.
“Oral storytelling was the main way to preserve traditions.”
compelling case
A strong, persuasive argument supported by clear reasons.
“Historians made a compelling case for restoring the old theatre.”
passive consumption
Watching or reading without actively thinking or engaging.
“Auto-play encourages passive consumption rather than active choice.”

5. Education & Lifelong Learning

4 Part 4 Prompts
Question 5.1
Is a university degree still worth the time and financial investment it requires today?
Angle to explore: vocational alternatives and debt vs analytical depth and networks.
“For regulated professions like medicine or law, it's non-negotiable, but in many practical fields, apprenticeships can offer a much faster, debt-free start.”
Question 5.2
Should schools focus more on practical life skills than traditional academic subjects?
Angle to explore: financial literacy and communication vs foundational knowledge.
“Ideally it shouldn't be an either-or choice; teaching personal finance alongside history or science equips students far better for adulthood.”
Question 5.3
Why do many adults stop learning new skills once they enter full-time employment?
Angle to explore: time pressure, fear of looking like a beginner, and comfort zones.
“Apart from sheer exhaustion after work, I think many adults dislike the feeling of being a novice again after years of competence in their own job.”
Question 5.4
How can teachers encourage critical thinking in an age of instant answers?
Angle to explore: questioning sources, debating, and process over memorisation.
“If students can look up any fact in two seconds, the classroom has to shift from testing recall to asking why an argument holds up or falls apart.”

Topic Vocabulary Bank

8 C1 Phrases
rote learning
Memorising facts through repetition without necessarily understanding them.
“Rote learning helps with dates, not with original problem-solving.”
vocational training
Education that prepares someone directly for a specific trade or craft.
“Strong vocational training is vital for engineering and healthcare.”
lifelong learning
Continuing to study and develop new skills throughout adulthood.
“Fast-changing industries make lifelong learning a necessity.”
critical literacy
The ability to evaluate sources, spot bias, and question claims.
“Critical literacy is essential when reading online news.”
step out of one's comfort zone
Try something unfamiliar that carries a risk of making mistakes.
“Speaking a foreign language forces you to step out of your comfort zone.”
transferable skills
Abilities (like communication or analysis) useful across many different jobs.
“Writing clear reports is one of the best transferable skills.”
an either-or choice
A situation forced into only two options when both could coexist.
“Theory and practice shouldn't be treated as an either-or choice.”
broaden one's horizons
Expand your range of knowledge, experience, and perspective.
“Studying abroad can genuinely broaden your horizons.”

6. Travel, Tourism & Globalisation

4 Part 4 Prompts
Question 6.1
Does mass tourism destroy the very authenticity that travellers are looking for?
Angle to explore: local shops replaced by souvenir chains vs economic support for regions.
“There's definitely a paradox there: once a quiet village goes viral, the influx of visitors often drives up rents and pushes out the local life people came to see.”
Question 6.2
Should popular destinations limit the number of visitors they allow each year?
Angle to explore: protecting fragile sites vs keeping travel affordable and open.
“Timed entry or visitor caps make complete sense for fragile historic centres, though pricing systems need to be careful not to turn travel into an elitist privilege.”
Question 6.3
Can you genuinely understand another culture through short holidays, or only by living there?
Angle to explore: surface impressions vs navigating daily routines, bureaucracy, and language.
“A week's holiday gives you a taste of the food and architecture, but you only really grasp a culture when you have to deal with its everyday routines.”
Question 6.4
How has budget travel changed the way people view the world?
Angle to explore: democratised exploration vs treating destinations as disposable checklists.
“Cheap flights made international travel accessible to millions for the first time, though it has also encouraged a rushed, checklist style of sightseeing.”

Topic Vocabulary Bank

8 C1 Phrases
over-tourism
Excessive numbers of tourists harming local life and the environment.
“Venice and Barcelona have introduced measures to curb over-tourism.”
off the beaten track
In a quiet place far from standard tourist routes.
“We preferred exploring small villages off the beaten track.”
sustainable tourism
Travel that respects local communities, wildlife, and resources.
“Travelling out of season is a simple way to support sustainable tourism.”
an influx of visitors
A sudden arrival of large numbers of people.
“Coastal towns rely heavily on the summer influx of visitors.”
tourist trap
An overpriced place designed mainly to take money from tourists.
“Restaurants right beside famous monuments are often tourist traps.”
local economy
The businesses, jobs, and trade within a specific town or region.
“Family-run guesthouses put money directly back into the local economy.”
cultural immersion
Deeply experiencing another way of life rather than observing from outside.
“Staying with a host family offers genuine cultural immersion.”
broaden perspective
Help you understand different viewpoints and ways of living.
“Even short trips can broaden your perspective if you stay curious.”
