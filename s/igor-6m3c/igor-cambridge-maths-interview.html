Cambridge Maths Interview: Thinking Aloud in English

Cambridge admissions interviews are short, interactive supervisions. Tutors are not looking for silent mental arithmetic; they want to hear how you test ideas, respond to hints, and justify each step in clear spoken English.

Opening Icebreaker: AI, Primes & the Riemann Hypothesis (20 Nov 2026)

Just five days ago, mathematics hit a major headline. Most Maths interviews jump straight to problems, though a topical icebreaker like this is a great warm-up for talking about what makes a proof convincing.

Breaking Maths Context
What Happened · 20 Nov 2026

Claimed Lean 4 Proof Sparks Debate

Mathematician Levent Alpöge and Anthropic’s Claude released a formal Lean 4 codebase claiming to prove Bernhard Riemann’s 1859 Hypothesis (governing prime distribution). Kernel verifiers (Lean FRO and Mathlib) confirmed the code compiles, though the Clay Institute and human peer reviewers have not accepted it yet.

Why It Matters to Maths

Verified vs. Understood

Prime distribution sits at the heart of number theory and cryptography. Yet the proof runs to 175,187 files (~17 million lines of code) with no human-readable paper yet, leaving mathematicians debating what “verified but not understood” means.

Why It Matters Today

Why Explaining Aloud Counts

If machines can compile millions of formal lines faster than humans can follow them, a Cambridge mathematician’s real value lies in intuition, insight, and explaining why a step works clearly aloud.

2-Minute Warm-Up Debate Prompt

"If a computer verifies a 17-million-line proof in Lean that no human mathematician can currently follow end to end, does that count as solving the Riemann Hypothesis, or is understanding still essential?"

How a Cambridge Mathematics Interview Works

Typically 2 separate interviews (often online for international applicants) of around 20 to 30 minutes each, using a shared virtual whiteboard while speaking aloud.

25-Minute Blueprint
Minutes 0 to 2 · Opening

Brief Settling-In

Minimal small talk. Unlike humanities interviews, Maths tutors rarely linger on your personal statement; they move straight into live reasoning.

Minutes 2 to 22 · Core

2 to 3 Escalating Problems

Starts with an accessible warm-up, then pushes until you get stuck on purpose so the tutor can drop a hint and watch how quickly you adapt.

Minutes 22 to 25 · Close

Quick Wrap-Up

Usually just a brief courtesy chance to ask one concise question at the end, so keep it calm, genuine, and short.

What Tutors Reward

Teachability & Audible Logic

Listening closely to a hint, starting with small cases before seeing the whole proof, stating assumptions explicitly, and explaining why a dead end failed.

What Does Not Matter

No Rehearsed Speeches

Memorised personal-statement scripts, showy vocabulary, or sitting in 45 seconds of silence trying to produce a flawless final answer in your head.

The 10 Supervision Habits

Treat the interview like a live conversation at a whiteboard, not a silent written exam.

Supervision Code
01
Keep the 10-second rule: Never calculate silently for more than 10 seconds. Say what you are checking right now.
02
Ban the word "obviously": Every step needs a spoken reason, even when the algebra feels simple to you.
03
State your plan before writing: Announce your strategy in one sentence before touching the whiteboard.
04
Take hints cleanly: Pause, acknowledge the nudge ("Good point, let me check..."), and run with it immediately.
05
Test the 4 universal edge cases: Check x = 0, n = 1, negative numbers, and very large values.
06
Diagnose dead ends out loud: If you cross something out, explain clearly why that approach broke down.
07
Pause for plain English: Every couple of minutes, summarise what you have proved without symbols.
08
Stay steady under challenge: When asked "Are you sure about that sign?", check calmly rather than rushing.
09
Read neutral faces calmly: Interviewers stay deadpan by design; a quiet "Okay, keep going" is normal.
10
Move on without regret: If a tutor cuts a problem short after 7 minutes, reset cleanly for the next one.

Round 1: The Mutilated Chessboard (Invariants)

Visual proof without algebra. Narrate your logic clearly from the very first sentence.

5:00 Timer · Logic & Parity
Take a standard 8 × 8 chessboard and remove two opposite corner squares (leaving 62 squares).

Can you tile all 62 remaining squares using 31 standard 2 × 1 dominoes with no overlaps and nothing hanging off the board?

Follow-Up Stress Questions

  • "Before checking the whole board, what property does any single 2 × 1 domino always preserve?"
  • "What changes if I remove two adjacent corners along the same edge instead?"
  • "Can you compress the entire proof into two clean spoken sentences?"
Checkpoint Hint (Small Nudge)
Check the colours of the two missing corners. Are they the same colour or opposite colours? Now compare that with the colours covered by a single domino.
Complete Proof & Plain-English Walkthrough
Answer: Impossible.
Plain-English Idea: On an 8 × 8 board, opposite corners are always the same colour (here, both white). Removing them leaves 32 black squares and only 30 white squares. Every 2 × 1 domino lies on two touching squares, so it must cover exactly 1 black and 1 white square. Therefore, 31 dominoes would always cover 31 black and 31 white squares, which can never match 32 and 30.
Black left: 32 White left: 30

Round 2: Divisibility & Exhaustive Cases

Practise announcing your factorisation and walking through every case systematically.

5:00 Timer · Number Theory
Part A: Prove that for every integer n, n3 − n is divisible by 6.

Part B: Can n2 + 1 ever be divisible by 3 for any integer n?

Follow-Up Stress Questions

  • "In Part A, why does divisibility by both 2 and 3 guarantee divisibility by 6? Would the same logic work for 4 and 6 giving 24?"
  • "How do you know your three cases in Part B cover every integer, including negative numbers?"
  • "What if we replace 3 with 5 in Part B? Can n2 + 1 be divisible by 5?"
Checkpoint Hint (Small Nudge)
For Part A, factorise n3 − n into three brackets. For Part B, write any integer n by its remainder when divided by 3: 3k, 3k + 1, or 3k + 2.
Complete Proof & Plain-English Walkthrough
Part A (Plain English): Factorising gives (n − 1) · n · (n + 1), which is three consecutive whole numbers in a row. Any three numbers in a row must include at least one even number (multiple of 2) and exactly one multiple of 3. Because 2 and 3 share no common factor (they are coprime), multiplying them means the product is always a multiple of 6.

Part B (Plain English): No. Every whole number leaves a remainder of 0, 1, or 2 when divided by 3. Squaring those remainders gives 0, 1, or 4 (which leaves remainder 1). Adding 1 gives remainders of 1 or 2, never 0. (Bonus check: for 5, YES, because 22 + 1 = 5.)
Click a sample integer to inspect both expressions.

Round 3: Live Curve Sketching Studio

A classic Cambridge staple: deduce the shape out loud before sketching directly onto the axes.

5:00 Timer · Interactive Sketch Pad
Sketch the curve y = x e−x for all real numbers x.

Talk through four checks aloud before drawing: (1) intercepts, (2) behaviour as x → +∞ and x → −∞, (3) turning points, and (4) where the slope changes concavity.

Follow-Up Stress Questions

  • "As x becomes huge and positive, x gets big while e−x gets tiny. Why does the exponential win?"
  • "How do you prove x = 1 is a maximum rather than a minimum or flat inflection?"
  • "Does the curve ever touch or cross the x-axis again after the origin?"
Checkpoint Hint (Small Nudge)
Differentiate with the product rule to get dy/dx = (1 − x)e−x. Notice that e−x is always strictly positive, so the sign of y matches the sign of x.
Complete Proof & Plain-English Walkthrough
Plain-English Shape:
• Crosses axes: Only at (0, 0), because e−x is never zero.
• Left side (x negative): Both pieces pull downward fast, so the curve plunges steeply to −∞.
• Peak: dy/dx = (1 − x)e−x = 0 at x = 1, giving (1, 1/e) ≈ (1, 0.37). Confirmed as a maximum because dy/dx flips from positive to negative across x = 1 (or d2y/dx2 = (x − 2)e−x = −1/e < 0).
• Right tail (x very large): Exponential decay beats linear growth, so the curve bends through an inflection at (2, 2/e2) ≈ (2, 0.27) and glides toward 0 without ever touching the x-axis.
Draw directly on the grid with mouse or stylus, then compare with the exact curve.

Challenge A: The Boeing 747 Tennis-Ball Fermi Challenge

Pure structured estimation in plain English. No algebra required: state every assumption transparently and justify your order of magnitude.

Fermi Estimation · 5:00
Step 1 & 2

Define the Question & Volume

Clarify first: passenger cabin only, or cargo hold and wings too? Model the fuselage as a rough cylinder (about 60 to 70 m long, 6 m wide).

Step 3 & 4

Ball Size & Air Gaps

A tennis ball is roughly 6.5 cm across. Spheres leave air gaps (roughly 50% to 70% of space is filled, or simply treat each ball as a 6.5 cm cube), and seats/galleys take space too.

Plain-English Sanity Check (Order of Magnitude)
Rough cylinder volume: π × 32 × 60 ≈ 1,700 m3 (the passenger cabin alone is roughly 800 to 1,000 m3).
One tennis ball: Volume of a 6.5 cm sphere is about 145 cm3 (~6,900 balls/m3 with zero gaps), or treating each ball as a 6.5 cm cube (~275 cm3) gives ~3,600 to 4,500 balls/m3 once gaps are included.
Consistent answer range: Around 2 million to 4 million for a furnished passenger cabin (removing ~30% to 50% for seats and galleys), and around 6 million to 8 million for an empty 1,700 m3 cylinder. Any clear answer in the low-to-mid millions backed by explicit assumptions passes cleanly.

Challenge B: "Explain It Without Symbols" Roulette

Draw a famous mathematical idea and explain why it is true in 90 seconds of crystal-clear English, with zero formulas on screen.

Non-Specialist Test
Card 1 of 5 · Classic Proof Intuition
Why Are There Infinitely Many Prime Numbers?

Imagine someone claims they have written down the complete, final list of every prime number that exists. Explain in plain English how Euclid proves their list must be missing at least one prime.

Core Idea: Multiply every prime on their supposedly complete list together, then add 1. That new number leaves a remainder of 1 whenever you divide by any prime on the list. So either the new number is itself a brand-new prime, or it is divisible by some prime that was not on the list. Either way, the list was incomplete.

Interview Composure

Scenario 2: The interviewer gives a hint, and you still do not immediately see where it leads.
