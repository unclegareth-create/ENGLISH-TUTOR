Abitur Analytical Masterclass: Tasks 1, 2 & Sprachmittlung

Transitioning from Cambridge CPE mechanics to long-form analytical academic writing. The German Abitur allocates 40% to Content (Inhalt) and 60% to Language (Sprache). Here is the complete blueprint to secure 14–15 points (Sehr Gut).

1 The Two Pillars • Part 1 & Part 2 Structure

Part 1: Text Analysis & Writing (70%)

Task 1: Comprehension (Outline / Summarize) • Objective synthesis of the primary text in your own words. Strictly no quotes; no personal commentary (2–3 paragraphs).

Task 2: Analysis • Examination of rhetorical & stylistic devices, argument structure, and narrative perspective. Explaining the exact function on the reader.

Task 3: Evaluation / Re-creation • Discursive essay (Option A) or functional text like a speech or article (Option B) connecting text themes to broader curriculum (globalization, politics, media).

Part 2: Language Mediation / Sprachmittlung (30%)

The Objective: Transferring vital information from a German source text into a coherent English text for a specific target audience.

The Core Rule: It is explicitly not a translation. You must filter out irrelevant anecdotes, adapt register to the target scenario (e.g. email, report, or briefing), and unpack German cultural idioms.

2 The 15-Point Grading Standard

Points Grade Descriptor & Quality Benchmark
13 – 15 pts Sehr Gut (Grade 1 / A) Outstanding performance. Flawless structure, advanced syntactic variety (clefts, inversions, participle clauses), precise analytical vocabulary, and deep contextual insights.
10 – 12 pts Gut (Grade 2 / B) Solid performance. Strong argumentation and language control, but slightly less stylistic flair or nuance in contextualising rhetorical effects.
7 – 9 pts Befriedigend (Grade 3 / C) Satisfactory. Meets basic requirements; contains occasional language errors or superficial "device-spotting" without explaining real functional impact.
4 – 6 pts Ausreichend (Grade 4 / D) Sufficient baseline. Core message understandable but heavily marred by mechanical errors, repetitive syntax, or missing arguments.

3 Analytical Function Verbs (Banish "The Author Shows")

Examiners penalise repetitive phrasing such as "The author uses X to make it interesting". Deploy these precise academic verbs to explain how rhetorical form generates meaning:

to underscore / highlight
Draws sharp focus to a crucial premise or statistical anomaly.
• "The author underscores the gravity of corporate negligence..."
to evoke / elicit
Creates an emotional resonance, atmosphere, or reader reaction.
• "The sensory imagery evokes a palpable sense of isolation..."
to juxtapose / polarize
Places contrasting concepts side-by-side to expose hypocrisy or disparity.
• "By juxtaposing corporate profits with worker deprivation..."
to reinforce / consolidate
Strengthens an argument through cumulative evidence or anaphora.
• "The rhythmic tricolon reinforces the speaker's core thesis..."
to allude to
Taps into shared cultural, literary, or historical authority.
• "The speech subtly alludes to Churchillian wartime rhetoric..."
to convey / delineate
Communicates complex abstract arguments with precision.
• "The narrative perspective delineates the internal moral conflict..."

4 The PEEL Analysis Formula

P • Point: Identify the specific rhetorical or stylistic device (e.g. inclusive pronouns, antithesis, metaphor).
E • Evidence: Integrate a succinct quote with line numbers (e.g. "blood, sweat, and tears" (l. 14)) seamlessly into your sentence.
E • Explanation: Detail the immediate cognitive or emotional effect on the audience (e.g. establishes solidarity, highlights contradiction).
L • Link: Connect this microscopic effect back to the macroscopic purpose or overarching thesis of the text.
