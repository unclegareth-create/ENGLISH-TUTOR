Part 2: The News Report vs. Hardware Reality

Step 02 · Critical Reading

Read the adapted news report below based on BBC News coverage (3 November 2026). Pay close attention to the highlighted phrases and how the order of information shapes the story.

iPhone Duo: Apple unsure when more of the foldable devices will be available

Adapted from BBC Technology News · Earnings Call Report

The new head of Apple says the company is working to get its new foldable iPhones available to more people after the £1,999 ($1,999) smartphone quickly sold out when it was released just over a week ago.

John Ternus, who took over as chief executive in September, said on Monday that he was "thrilled" at the early reaction to the iPhone Duo. While the costly phone immediately sold out, some buyers have seen their screens crack along the central fold.

Sabih Khan, Apple's chief operating officer, told financial analysts: "At this time, it's really hard to predict when supply and demand will be in balance."

Apple executives did not mention any hardware faults during the investor call. However, last week the company confirmed it would replace phones that were impacted by an issue that caused a "small number" of displays to crack along the inside hinge—though many retail stores currently lack replacement stock.

Meanwhile, Apple blamed "unexpected demand" for microprocessor shortages and pointed to the artificial intelligence data-centre boom for driving up memory costs—citing what executives earlier called a "thousand-year flood" in component costs after raising hardware prices across the board by roughly 20%. Despite those cost warnings, investors noted Apple's overall profit margins remain well north of 45%.

Part 3: The Corporate Spin Decoder

Step 03 · Pragmatics & Euphemism

In yours or your company's meetings, recognizing polite corporate hedging is a vital C1 skill. Translate each executive statement into plain, everyday English before revealing the model analysis.

1. "At this time, it is really hard to predict when supply and demand will be in balance."

Why do executives say "supply and demand will be in balance" instead of telling customers when the phone will be back in shops?

Plain-English Reality: "We don't know when you can buy one—or when we can replace your broken one."
Linguistic Device: Nominalisation & Economic Abstraction. By pairing "supply" with "demand", the speaker implies the shortage is caused by massive popularity rather than low factory yields, paused assembly lines, or replacement units eating up warehouse stock.
2. "...phones that were impacted by an issue that caused a 'small number' of displays to crack."

Look at the grammar: who or what is performing the action here?

Plain-English Reality: "Our hinge design puts too much stress on the folding glass, so the screens are snapping."
Linguistic Device: Agentless Passive + Vague Noun ("an issue"). Saying phones "were impacted by an issue" treats a design flaw like bad weather—nobody designed it, nobody built it, it simply happened from the outside.
3. Calling component price rises a "thousand-year flood" while raising retail prices by 20% (at 45%+ profit margins).

What effect does a natural-disaster metaphor have on an audience of buyers or journalists?

Plain-English Reality: "RAM and chip wafers cost us more this year, so we passed the entire bill onto customers to protect our 45% profit margin."
Linguistic Device: Force-Majeure Metaphor. Comparing supplier costs to a "thousand-year flood" frames a commercial pricing decision as an unavoidable act of nature.
